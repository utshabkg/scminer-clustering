## Stability-based selection of the number of clusters, and where it fails

**Shai Ben-David, Ulrike von Luxburg, Dávid Pál (2006).** A Sober Look at Clustering Stability. *Learning Theory: 19th Annual Conference on Learning Theory (COLT 2006), Lecture Notes in Computer Science vol. 4005, Springer, pp. 5-19*. https://doi.org/10.1007/11776420_4. Key `BenDavid2006Sober`.

- What it shows: For large samples, the stability of a clustering algorithm that minimises an objective is set by that objective alone. A unique global minimiser gives a stable algorithm. Several minimisers give an unstable one (proved when the data have a symmetry that swaps the minimisers, and stated as a conjecture in general). The authors conclude that stability is not a well suited tool for choosing k, because it reflects symmetries of the data that may have nothing to do with the number of clusters. Their examples include slightly asymmetric mixtures where k-means with the wrong k (3 when the answer is 2, or 2 when it is 3) is stable, and the uniform distribution on [0, 1], where every k is stable.
- Why it matters here: This is the theory behind finding (2). A setting can reproduce perfectly without being the right one, so a perfect stability score cannot separate candidate settings on its own. Cite it to explain why many settings tie at the top and why the tie-break, not stability, ends up choosing each method's setting.

**Ulrike von Luxburg (2010).** Clustering Stability: An Overview. *Foundations and Trends in Machine Learning 2(3):235-274*. https://doi.org/10.1561/2200000008 (preprint https://arxiv.org/abs/1007.1075). Key `vonLuxburg2010Stability`.

- What it shows: A survey of stability-based selection of k. It warns that if the data are perturbed too little, the algorithm keeps returning the same result and stability becomes trivial. Raw instability grows with k even on data with no clusters, so it has to be normalised, and with too few clusters both stability and instability can occur. The author also writes that no convincing empirical study compares the many implementation variants and that even normalisation has not been studied in enough detail.
- Why it matters here: Directly supports findings (2) and (3). Trivial stability under mild perturbation matches our flat top on small datasets. The open question about implementation variants (how to perturb, how to normalise, how to pick k from the scores) matches our result that the number of subsamples and the bootstrap design change the chosen setting.

**Jonas M. B. Haslbeck, Dirk U. Wulff (2020).** Estimating the number of clusters via a corrected clustering instability. *Computational Statistics 35(4):1879-1894*. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00180-020-00981-5 (preprint https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.07494). Key `Haslbeck2020Corrected`.

- What it shows: Shows that clustering instability depends on the distribution of cluster sizes, and so on k itself, whatever the true k is. In their example (the three-cluster problem of Fang and Wang 2012) the uncorrected instability path has a local minimum at the true k = 3 but falls below that value again for k > 23 (model-free) and k > 25 (model-based). The estimate therefore overshoots unless the candidate set is limited to small k, which is how earlier methods (Ben-Hur et al., Fang and Wang) avoided the problem. They propose a corrected instability that adjusts the clustering distance for cluster sizes.
- Why it matters here: The closest published support for finding (1). What a stability rule picks depends on which candidate values of k it is allowed to see, so a shared cap and a shared candidate grid are different conditions. It also gives a reason to report each method's candidate set when methods are compared.

**Yasin Şenbabaoğlu, George Michailidis, Jun Z. Li (2014).** Critical limitations of consensus clustering in class discovery. *Scientific Reports 4:6207*. https://doi.org/10.1038/srep06207 (PMID 25158761, PMC4145288). Key `Senbabaoglu2014Critical`.

- What it shows: Consensus clustering scores a setting by how often pairs of samples land in the same cluster across repeated subsampled runs. Using simulated data and real tumour expression data, the authors show that it splits randomly generated unimodal data into apparently stable clusters for a range of K, and that the common ways of reading its output, above all the delta K rule, do poorly at recovering the true K on data with known structure. They propose PAC (the proportion of ambiguously clustered pairs) and null data built from the observed gene-gene correlation structure.
- Why it matters here: chooseR computes its silhouette on the same kind of co-clustering frequency matrix, so this is the nearest genomics warning that high co-clustering stability does not prove real structure. It supports finding (2) and argues for checking a structure-free reference before trusting a stability-chosen setting, above all on small datasets.

**Asa Ben-Hur, André Elisseeff, Isabelle Guyon (2002).** A stability based method for discovering structure in clustered data. *Pacific Symposium on Biocomputing 2002, pp. 6-17*. https://doi.org/10.1142/9789812799623_0002 (PMID 11928511, PDF https://psb.stanford.edu/psb-online/proceedings/psb02/benhur.pdf). Key `BenHur2002Stability`.

- What it shows: Introduced subsampling stability for choosing k. Pairs of large subsamples are clustered, labels are compared on shared points, and the rule picks the largest k whose similarity distribution stays concentrated near 1. In their four-Gaussian example the scores for k = 2, 3 and 4 all sit near 1, so the answer comes from the largest-stable-k convention. They also cut dendrograms so that clusters are not singletons, which sharpens the transition between stable and unstable k.
- Why it matters here: Shows that flat tops were there from the start and that the classic rule settles them by convention, close to chooseR's preference for the highest resolution that passes its threshold. Supports finding (2). The singleton handling is an early case of a design detail that changes the answer (finding 3).

**Robert Tibshirani, Guenther Walther (2005).** Cluster Validation by Prediction Strength. *Journal of Computational and Graphical Statistics 14(3):511-528*. https://doi.org/10.1198/106186005X59243. Key `Tibshirani2005Prediction`.

- What it shows: Prediction strength treats clustering as classification. The data are split into training and test parts (repeated cross-validation, two folds in their simulations), each part is clustered, and the score is how well the training centroids predict which test points share a cluster, taking the worst test cluster. By construction ps(1) = 1. The authors estimate k as the largest k that maximises ps(k), and in practice as the largest k with ps(k) at or above 0.8 to 0.9 (in their simulations ps(k) plus its standard error at or above 0.8).
- Why it matters here: Another classic rule where a threshold and a largest-k tie-break do the deciding. It gives context for finding (2) and for chooseR's bootstrap threshold, and it is a reminder that the trivial setting is always perfectly stable.

**Tilman Lange, Volker Roth, Mikio L. Braun, Joachim M. Buhmann (2004).** Stability-Based Validation of Clustering Solutions. *Neural Computation 16(6):1299-1323*. https://doi.org/10.1162/089976604773717621. Key `Lange2004Stability`.

- What it shows: Defines stability as the expected disagreement between the clustering of one sample and the labels that a classifier trained on another sample's clustering predicts, read as a classification risk. The chosen k minimises this risk. Because the range of the score depends on k, the scores are normalised by those of random labelings (described this way in von Luxburg 2010 and in the 2002 NIPS version).
- Why it matters here: Shows that raw stability scores are not on a common scale across k. This matters when methods are compared over different candidate sets (finding 1) and when methods are ranked by how stable their chosen settings are.

## How tuning and selection change benchmark rankings

**Siddhartha Mishra, Nicholas Monath, Michael Boratko, Ariel Kobren, Andrew McCallum (2022).** An Evaluative Measure of Clustering Methods Incorporating Hyperparameter Sensitivity. *Proceedings of the AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence 36(7), pages 7788-7796*. https://doi.org/10.1609/aaai.v36i7.20747. Key `mishra2022evaluative`.

- What it shows: Scores clustering methods by the expected best ARI reached after t hyperparameter trials, once under random search (an uninformed user) and once under Bayesian optimisation (an expert), for mini-batch k-means, BIRCH, DBSCAN, HDBSCAN and HAC on over 50 datasets. The winner depends on how much and how cleverly each method is tuned: with enough tuning BIRCH overtakes HAC on the generic datasets, and mini-batch k-means can beat HAC even though HAC is far easier to tune. Rankings also shift between easy and hard datasets. One figure, for a single dataset (AGNews with GloVe embeddings), compares tuning the number of clusters with handing over the true count, and the text notes that HAC given the true count reaches its best score almost at once because only the linkage is left to tune.
- Why it matters here: Closest clustering precedent for finding (1). What a method may search over, and how it searches, changes the ranking. The paper still picks the best setting with ground-truth ARI, so it never tests a label-free rule like chooseR. That gap is where our paper sits. It also supports reporting rankings as a function of the selection procedure instead of as one number.

**Iven Van Mechelen, Anne-Laure Boulesteix, Rainer Dangl, Nema Dean, Christian Hennig, Friedrich Leisch, Douglas Steinley, Matthijs J. Warrens (2023).** A white paper on good research practices in benchmarking: The case of cluster analysis. *WIREs Data Mining and Knowledge Discovery 13(6), e1511*. https://doi.org/10.1002/widm.1511. Key `vanmechelen2023white`.

- What it shows: Reviews good practice for benchmarking clustering methods. Its recommendations call for a fair comparison in which no method is wrongly favoured or handicapped. Spelled out, that means the same prior information for every method, fair choices of tuning parameters, comparable compute, and the same time and expertise spent on choosing tuning parameters. It also asks authors to disclose every tuning value and seed, lists stability across tuning parameter choices as an evaluation criterion, and treats choosing the number of clusters as its own post-processing step.
- Why it matters here: The normative anchor for finding (1). A shared cap on cluster count does not meet its fairness standard when the candidate grids differ between methods. It also backs finding (3), because it asks that the details of the selection step be reported in full. Written by clustering methodologists, so it is the natural citation for the reliability framing.

**Xavier Bouthillier et al. (16 authors, last author Pascal Vincent) (2021).** Accounting for Variance in Machine Learning Benchmarks. *Proceedings of Machine Learning and Systems (MLSys) 3, pages 747-769*. https://proceedings.mlsys.org/paper_files/paper/2021/hash/0184b0cd3cfb185989f858a1d9f5c1eb-Abstract.html (arXiv:2103.03098). Key `bouthillier2021accounting`.

- What it shows: Models the whole benchmark pipeline, hyperparameter search included, on five deep learning tasks. Running 20 independent searches with each of random search, a perturbed grid search and Bayesian optimisation, they find the tuning procedure alone adds on average as much variance as weight initialisation. They point out that the range and step of a grid (powers of 2, powers of 10, steps of 0.25 or 0.5) are arbitrary and act as an uncontrolled source of variance. They recommend randomising as many sources of variation as possible, judging a win by the probability that one method beats the other in a single run, and using resampling such as out-of-bootstrap instead of one fixed test set.
- Why it matters here: Supports findings (2) and (3). The selection step is itself a noisy procedure, and its noise can be as large as the gaps being compared. The remark on arbitrary grid ranges and steps matches our finding that the candidate list, not only its cap, moves the result. Its advice suggests treating the chooseR pick as a random variable and repeating it over subsampling seeds before ranking methods.

**Ulrike von Luxburg, Robert C. Williamson, Isabelle Guyon (2012).** Clustering: Science or Art?. *Proceedings of ICML Workshop on Unsupervised and Transfer Learning, PMLR 27, pages 65-79*. https://proceedings.mlr.press/v27/luxburg12a.html. Key `vonluxburg2012clustering`.

- What it shows: Position paper arguing that clustering algorithms cannot be compared in a context-free way. It calls agreement with class labels a dangerous and misleading yardstick, since labels need not match cluster structure. It judges internal quality scores unsuitable for objective comparison across algorithms, because for any score that prefers one clustering another score can be built that prefers the other. Stability is described as a meta-criterion that handles statistical uncertainty without measuring how useful a clustering is.
- Why it matters here: Explains why a label-free selection rule is never neutral. The chooseR criterion (silhouette on co-clustering distance plus a stability threshold) is one internal score among many, and a different score could favour a different method. Its view of stability as a check on noise, not on usefulness, is consistent with finding (2), where many settings are perfectly stable and stability alone cannot pick one.

**Jesse Dodge, Suchin Gururangan, Dallas Card, Roy Schwartz, Noah A. Smith (2019).** Show Your Work: Improved Reporting of Experimental Results. *Proceedings of EMNLP-IJCNLP 2019, pages 2185-2194*. https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/D19-1224. Key `dodge2019show`.

- What it shows: Introduces expected validation performance, the expected best score as a function of the number of hyperparameter trials or of training time. On 5-class SST, logistic regression beats a CNN for budgets up to 10 trials and the CNN wins after that. Re-running published comparisons, they find several where the stated winner holds only for some budgets. In the ELMo sentiment comparison, for example, the published conclusion held only between about 6 hours and 1 day of compute, fine-tuning won at about 10 days, and plain GloVe was best under 2 hours.
- Why it matters here: Supports finding (1) from the supervised side. A ranking is conditional on the search each method receives, and it can flip when that search changes. Their fix, reporting the whole curve, suggests showing our rankings across candidate grids and selection settings instead of at one configuration.

**Mario Lucic, Karol Kurach, Marcin Michalski, Sylvain Gelly, Olivier Bousquet (2018).** Are GANs Created Equal? A Large-Scale Study. *Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 31 (NeurIPS 2018)*. https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2018/hash/e46de7e1bcaaced9a54f1e9d0d2f800d-Abstract.html (arXiv:1711.10337). Key `lucic2018gans`.

- What it shows: Large study of GANs under fixed compute budgets with wide hyperparameter searches. With enough search and random restarts most models reach similar FID, no model clearly dominates, a nominally bad model with a large budget can beat a good model with a small one, and with a limited budget a statistically significant comparison is out of reach. The authors argue for comparing distributions of results at a fixed budget, not the single best run.
- Why it matters here: Shows that gaps between methods can come from unequal search effort, the mechanism behind finding (1). Its advice to compare result distributions at a fixed budget fits how chooseR outcomes should be reported across seeds and grids. Its point that a small budget makes significant comparisons impossible is a useful parallel for our few-hundred-cell datasets, where flat stability scores leave little signal.

**Gábor Melis, Chris Dyer, Phil Blunsom (2018).** On the State of the Art of Evaluation in Neural Language Models. *International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR 2018)*. https://openreview.net/forum?id=ByJHuTgA- (arXiv:1707.05589). Key `melis2018state`.

- What it shows: Re-tunes LSTM, Recurrent Highway Network and NAS language models with large-scale black-box hyperparameter search under matched parameter budgets. Once tuning is controlled, plain LSTMs beat the newer architectures on Penn Treebank and Wikitext-2, against the published claims. The authors attribute the earlier claims to poorly controlled hyperparameter variation, check how far the tuner overfits noise, and ask that hyperparameter sensitivity be reported as part of a method's description.
- Why it matters here: A well-known case of a ranking that flipped once tuning was equalised, the same kind of reversal as in finding (1). Their request to report sensitivity supports our proposal to show how the chosen setting moves with selection details such as subsample count and bootstrap unit.

## Parameter choice in single-cell clustering benchmarks

**Pierre-Luc Germain, Anthony Sonrel, Mark D. Robinson (2020).** pipeComp, a general framework for the evaluation of computational pipelines, reveals performant single cell RNA-seq preprocessing tools. *Genome Biology 21:227*. https://doi.org/10.1186/s13059-020-02136-7. Key `germain2020pipecomp`.

- What it shows: The authors benchmarked whole scRNA-seq pipelines on labelled data and swept Seurat's resolution and number of dimensions. They report that "the number of clusters called is by far the most important determinant of the score" for ARI. After modelling dataset and Seurat's resolution, an ANOVA "attributed relatively little importance to the clustering method" and gave most of the variance to normalization and the number of dimensions. Seurat could always be coerced to the true cluster count through its resolution parameter. The scran graph clustering responded less to its parameters and sometimes never produced a partition with the right number of clusters. Because ARI is dominated by the cluster count, they also reported MI (which they note "can be biased towards methods producing higher resolution clustering"), per-population precision and ARI at the true number of clusters.
- Why it matters here: Strong support for finding (1). Which cluster counts a method can actually reach decides its score, so a shared cap does not give methods a level field. It also backs the reframing: part of what looks like an algorithm ranking is really a resolution effect. Useful when we explain why we report ARI and NMI at the selected setting and not only at the true k.

**Felix Raimundo, Celine Vallot, Jean-Philippe Vert (2020).** Tuning parameters of dimensionality reduction methods for single-cell RNA-seq analysis. *Genome Biology 21:212*. https://doi.org/10.1186/s13059-020-02128-7. Key `raimundo2020tuning`.

- What it shows: Over about 1.5 million runs on ten labelled datasets, the authors varied the parameters of five dimensionality reduction methods (scran, Seurat, ZinbWave, DCA, scVI) and scored k-means partitions at the true k by AMI. With default settings scran had the best mean AMI (0.840) and scVI the worst (0.560). With the best settings picked using the labels the order became ZinbWave 0.896, DCA 0.885, scVI 0.872, scran 0.868, Seurat 0.860. The authors warn this best-case figure may be over-optimistic for DCA and scVI, whose grids were two orders of magnitude larger. A silhouette heuristic that ignored the labels but was given the true number of clusters reached only 0.510 to 0.825 mean AMI, below the defaults for every method except ZinbWave. On the hardest immune mixtures it chose worse settings than the defaults for most methods, which the authors attribute to "overfitting a bad initial clustering".
- Why it matters here: The closest precedent to our reframing. How settings are chosen (defaults, oracle tuning or a label-free silhouette rule) reorders the methods. It supports our finding that the selection rule, not just the algorithm, drives the ranking, and it shows a silhouette-based rule failing in practice. Their rule still used the true number of clusters, so our chooseR setting is harder.

**Angelo Duò, Mark D. Robinson, Charlotte Soneson (2018).** A systematic performance evaluation of clustering methods for single-cell RNA-seq data. *F1000Research 7:1141 (version 1 2018, version 3 2020)*. https://doi.org/10.12688/f1000research.15666.3. Key `duo2018systematic`.

- What it shows: The study ran 14 clustering methods on nine real and three simulated datasets. Each method was run over k from 2 to 10 or 15, and Seurat over resolutions giving a similar range, with five runs per setting. No method reached its own best ARI at the annotated k on every dataset, though most peaked at or near it. FlowSOM was the exception and needed many more clusters to peak. Among methods that estimate k themselves (SC3, CIDR, ascend, SAFE, TSCAN) the estimate was usually close, but ascend and CIDR tended to underestimate and SC3 and TSCAN to overestimate. At the true k, SC3 and Seurat were the strongest methods, and SC3 was still the best at its estimated k. In a reply to a referee the authors add that SC3 also scores best when each method is run at its best k.
- Why it matters here: Shows that ARI comparisons depend on the k at which each method is scored and that methods peak at different k, which bears on finding (1). It is also a fair counterpoint: here the top of the ranking held up across k regimes, so reversals like ours are not universal and need to be shown case by case.

**Monika Krzak, Yordan Raykov, Alexis Boukouvalas, Luisa Cutillo, Claudia Angelini (2019).** Benchmark and Parameter Sensitivity Analysis of Single-Cell RNA Sequencing Clustering Methods. *Frontiers in Genetics 10:1253*. https://doi.org/10.3389/fgene.2019.01253. Key `krzak2019benchmark`.

- What it shows: The authors benchmarked several scRNA-seq clustering methods on real and simulated data while varying preprocessing, dimension reduction and how each method is run. They state that "the performance of several methods strongly depends on their parameter settings". For example, Seurat was sensitive to the number of input dimensions and SIMLR to whether k was estimated. Supplying the true k usually gave higher ARI than letting methods estimate it, and many methods over or under estimated k in a consistent way. They found no "golden rule" for parameters and advise checking how robust a partition is to parameter changes.
- Why it matters here: Supports findings (2) and (3) in general terms. Small choices made by the user move the result, and estimating k is a main source of error. It gives a published basis for our claim that implementation details of the selection rule deserve to be reported.

**Owen Visser, Somnath Datta (2025).** Integrating Multiple Clustering Techniques and Performance Measures via Ranking for scRNA-Seq Data. *Statistics in Medicine 44(28-30):e70331*. https://doi.org/10.1002/sim.70331. Key `visser2025integrating`.

- What it shows: The authors compared 12 pipelines (PCA or t-SNE followed by Ward hierarchical clustering, k-means, Louvain or Leiden, plus RaceID, two pcaReduce variants and CIDR) across a range of cluster counts and Louvain or Leiden settings. They used five labelled datasets of 48 to 301 cells and one unlabelled mouse bone marrow set. Louvain and Leiden settings were kept only if they gave a cluster count inside the same candidate range. Each setting was scored with stability measures (APN, AD, ADM), the label-based BSI and BHI, and internal measures (connectivity, silhouette, Dunn, IGP). The measures disagreed on the best cluster count and often picked an end of the candidate range. On the 48-cell Biase data (range 2 to 7) AD picked 7 and ADM picked 2, and the authors write that "no measure can be relied upon alone to give consistent results". Two rank aggregation schemes also picked different winners on the 301-cell Pollen data: pcaReduce with statistical merging at 3 clusters under scheme A and PCA with k-means at 10 clusters under scheme B.
- Why it matters here: Direct support for findings (2) and (3). On small gold standard datasets the choice among stability and internal criteria decides both the selected cluster count and the winning method. It also shows that label-free selection of method and setting is an active topic in the statistics literature.

**Saskia Freytag, Luyi Tian, Ingrid Lönnstedt, Milica Ng, Melanie Bahlo (2018).** Comparison of clustering tools in R for medium-sized 10x Genomics single-cell RNA-sequencing data. *F1000Research 7:1297 (version 2)*. https://doi.org/10.12688/f1000research.15809.2. Key `freytag2018comparison`.

- What it shows: The authors compared clustering tools in R on 10x Genomics data with gold standard (three cell lines) and silver standard (PBMC) labels, running every tool at its default parameters. Most tools estimated k themselves, and the estimates varied widely. About half overestimated the three cell line groups, and SC3 and RaceID2 returned more than 20 clusters. Partitions from different tools had little in common. The authors list defaults-only parameters and no test of robustness to parameter changes as limitations.
- Why it matters here: Shows what happens when each tool uses its own built-in rule to pick k. The comparison then mixes the algorithm with its selection rule, which is the confound our finding (1) isolates. Good context for why we hold the selection rule fixed across methods.

**Vladimir Yu Kiselev, Tallulah S. Andrews, Martin Hemberg (2019).** Challenges in unsupervised clustering of single-cell RNA-seq data. *Nature Reviews Genetics 20(5):273-282*. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-018-0088-9. Key `kiselev2019challenges`.

- What it shows: A review of unsupervised clustering for scRNA-seq. It argues that clustering is central to finding putative cell types but hard for computational reasons, for reasons tied to the data, and because clusters are hard to interpret and annotate. Its worked example on the Deng embryo data shows k-means at k = 5, a hierarchical tree that can be cut at several heights, and Louvain on graphs with 5 or 10 neighbours. More neighbours "indirectly decreases the resolution of graph-based clustering".
- Why it matters here: Background for the introduction. The right k or resolution is not fixed by the data alone, and hidden settings such as neighbourhood size shift the effective resolution. That matters when we compare graph-based Leiden with k-means and MICA at what looks like the same setting.
