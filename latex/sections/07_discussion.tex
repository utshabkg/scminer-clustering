\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
The results answer the main question only in part.

\textbf{What is now answered.} On the nine small datasets MICA's reported advantage survives in direction under this rule. Offered the same
cluster counts as the baseline, one setting each, MICA has the higher mean under both readings of the stability
rule at every seed, and, at one seed, under the silhouette rule when it is scored in the 50 principal components, a lead that
does not hold in MICA's own embedding. 
How large the advantage is still depends on choices that have nothing to do with either method, the reading of the
rule, its threshold and the subsample draw, and the lead rests on five of the nine datasets. On PBMC14K the matched
runs leave the methods tied under the plain maximum, and a 0.02 change in the threshold reverses which leads, so the large-dataset evidence supports no ranking and does not justify MICA's selection cost there, 93 minutes against 7.5.

On matched candidates both fall below the label-fixed means, MICA further, and the gap shrinks from 0.195 to 0.119 or 0.020. Beyond the general concern about tuning on labels \cite{ullmann2023overoptimistic}, this gives a specific measurement for MICA and a failure mode any label-free
comparison should check for: whether the rule chose a cluster count the other method could not have produced. A cap
on the range does not prevent it, and matching the counts does.

\textbf{Why it happens.} The matched runs separate two sources of ambiguity. The first is the candidates, which explained the reversal. The
second is ties at the top of the stability scores, and it is mostly MICA's. The two readings break such a tie in
opposite directions, the plain maximum toward fewer clusters and chooseR's toward more, which is why they move MICA's
result further apart. 

That the flat top sits in the smallest datasets favours a small-sample explanation over the mode, though nine datasets
cannot separate size from the datasets themselves. We had suspected that MICA's partitions are too reproducible for
reproducibility to discriminate, because its distance between two cells does not change when the sample does. That may
matter most when cells are few, but we have not tested it in isolation.

\textbf{What it changes for someone using these tools.} Within what we tested, a user should do five things differently. Compare methods on the same cluster counts with one
setting each, rather than only under the same cap, and check whether a method's chosen count is one the other could have
produced. On a dataset of a few hundred cells, do not let a stability score choose MICA's number of clusters on its own,
and if most settings tie at the maximum or two readings of the same scores disagree, treat the rule as having made no
choice. Report the chosen cluster count beside the score, which exposes both failures at once. Do not read the strength of
MICA's default of four clusters as evidence that it generalises, since tuning on this benchmark's labels picks the same
value. And judge a faster neighbour graph by its clustering and memory, not by recall and time alone: ours helps only when time is short and memory is spare.

\textbf{Limitations.} The two pipelines differ in distance, embedding and clustering step, so the comparison does not isolate mutual information. It uses rules we chose on datasets from a single benchmark. The
matched design was fixed before its runs but after the post hoc check that prompted it, and one setting per count is one
reasonable choice among several. Even the matched grids reach 14 to 19 of the 19 counts per run, and 2 and 3 clusters are
never compared on PBMC14K, the only large dataset in the selection comparison. We use 25 subsamples where chooseR specifies 100. We reimplemented chooseR with a simpler bootstrap and, on PBMC14K, a 3,000-cell score (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}) rather than run its R code, and since the choice is sensitive to a shift of 0.02, a discrepancy in our reading could
propagate to every stability result here, the matched comparison included. The graph comparison covers two datasets, lets
the labels pick the resolution, and compares numpy against compiled code, so it bounds the cost of the two graphs on this
machine rather than ranking the algorithms in general.
