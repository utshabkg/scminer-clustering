\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\subsection{What is now answered}
Part of MICA's reported advantage survives. It has the higher mean under three of the four
rules, by small margins, and when its saved scores are restricted after the fact to the counts
the baseline produced it leads under both readings, 0.772 to 0.632 and 0.665 to 0.635. The
qualification is the result. How large the advantage is, and under the plain maximum which
method leads, depends on choices that have nothing to do with either method: the tie-break, the threshold, the
subsample draw, and above all which cluster counts each method's grid offers. On PBMC14K both
methods choose at the edge of their grids, and a 0.02 change in the threshold reverses which
leads. A benchmark reporting a single label-free rule on unmatched grids would appear to be
making a statement about the methods when much of it would be a statement about the rule and
its candidates.

This is a different claim from the one we set out to test. It is weaker in that we cannot give
a user a single ranking, and stronger in that it identifies a measurable failure mode any
label-free comparison should check for: whether the rule chose a cluster count the other method
could not have produced.

\subsection{Why it happens}
What we can support is narrower than the mechanism we proposed. We suggested that MICA's
partitions are too reproducible for reproducibility to discriminate, because its distance is
computed per cell pair and does not move when the sample changes. That property is real, and
we prove and test it as an exact identity, but it holds in both of MICA's modes, and on a
common range of cluster counts neither mode's two readings disagreed, so it cannot by itself
produce the ambiguity. The $k$-means step that follows is not deterministic either: at a fixed
$k$ MICA's ARI moves by as much as 0.267 across seeds, on Buettner at $k=7$.

The evidence points instead to a flat top combined with the candidates. On the datasets where
the two readings disagree most, MICA's stability is flat at its maximum across much of the
range: 82 percent of its settings score exactly 1.0 on Yan, 70 percent on Pollen and 47 on
Chung, against 5 percent on average for the baseline. The two readings break that tie in
opposite directions, the plain maximum toward fewer clusters and chooseR's toward more, so they
are sent to opposite ends of whatever range is on offer. MICA's grid offers both ends on every
dataset and the baseline's usually does not, which is why MICA's readings drift further apart,
0.197 per dataset in absolute terms against 0.067. Held to a common range, the span of tied cluster counts falls from 7.3
to 4.1 against the baseline's 2.7. A difference remains, and we have measured it rather than
explained it.

The competing explanation we flagged in Assignment 2, that the ambiguity is a small-sample
effect, remains open. Our mode test held the size fixed at 2,500 cells, but the confound in the
candidate range means it speaks to the grids rather than to size, and five of the nine small
datasets have only 124 to 317 cells, which no run here reaches.

\subsection{What it changes for someone using these tools}
Within what we tested, a user should do four things differently. Compare methods on the same
cluster counts rather than only under the same cap, and check whether a method's chosen count is
one the other could have produced, since the reversal came from counts it could not. Do not let
one stability score choose MICA's number of clusters on its own, and if two readings of the same
scores disagree, or most settings tie at the maximum, treat the rule as having made no choice. Report the chosen cluster
count beside the score, which exposes both failures at once. And judge a faster neighbour graph
by the clustering it yields and by its memory, not by recall and time alone.

\subsection{Limitations}
The comparison covers one method against one baseline, under rules we chose, on datasets from a
single benchmark. The candidate grids were fixed before scoring but do not reach the same
cluster counts, and we correct for that after the fact by restricting MICA's saved scores to
the counts the baseline produced. Extending both grids to cover 2 to 20 clusters on every
dataset and rerunning would be cleaner, and it comes first in our plan. The mode test runs at a
single size on a single dataset. Some datasets carry labels derived by clustering the same
data, which flatters methods resembling the original pipeline, and PBMC14K is the only large
dataset in the selection comparison. We use 25 subsamples where chooseR specifies 100, which widens the bootstrap intervals
and lowers the threshold, and we report an observed range over three seeds rather than a
confidence interval, because three runs do not support one. Our implementation follows
chooseR's published description rather than running the authors' R code, and since the
threshold check shows the choice is sensitive at the level a bootstrap difference could
produce, a discrepancy in our reading would propagate to every result in
Table~\ref{tab:rules}. The graph comparison covers two datasets, lets the labels pick the
resolution so that only the graph differs, and compares numpy against compiled code, so it
bounds the cost of the two graphs on this machine rather than ranking the algorithms in
general.
