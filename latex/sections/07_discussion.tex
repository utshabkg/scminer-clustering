\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\subsection{What is now answered}
On the nine small datasets MICA's reported advantage survives in direction under this rule,
and the matched runs show it more cleanly than the runs as first specified. Offered the same
cluster counts as the baseline, one setting each, MICA has the higher mean under both readings
of the stability rule at every seed, 0.662 against 0.543 and 0.473 against 0.452, and under the
silhouette rule on the same candidates, 0.593 against 0.478. It also has the higher mean under
the shipped defaults and the label-informed transfer, but these add no support: the defaults
favour MICA for reasons of their own (Appendix~\ref{app:secondary}), and the transfer picks
MICA's default (Section~\ref{sec:transfer}). The reversal we first saw came from the candidates.
The baseline's grid could not produce the coarse partitions the plain maximum drifts to, and
once it could, its score fell below MICA's. How large the advantage is
still depends on choices that have nothing to do with either method, the reading of the rule,
its threshold and the subsample draw, and the lead rests on five of the nine datasets.
On PBMC14K the matched runs leave the methods tied under the plain maximum, and a 0.02 change
in the threshold reverses which leads, so the large-dataset evidence supports no ranking.

This is a narrower claim than the one we set out to test, and it comes with a failure mode any
label-free comparison should check for: whether the rule chose a cluster count the other method
could not have produced. A cap on the range does not prevent it, and matching the counts does.

\subsection{Why it happens}
The matched runs separate two sources of ambiguity. The first is the candidates, which
explained the reversal. The second belongs to MICA. Its stability scores are flat at the top,
and matching the candidates leaves that unchanged, with 40 percent of its settings tied with
the best against 14 percent for the baseline. The two readings break such a tie in opposite
directions, the plain maximum toward fewer clusters and chooseR's toward more, which is why they
move MICA's result further apart, 0.197 per dataset against 0.118.

Buettner aside, the flat top is concentrated in the smallest datasets. On the five with 124 to 317 cells
MICA's tied settings span 10.6 cluster counts against the baseline's 4.2, and on Yan 81 percent
of its settings reproduce perfectly. On the four larger datasets the spans are 3.3 against 1.3,
and on 2,500 PBMC14K cells neither of MICA's modes shows it. That favours the small-sample
explanation we flagged in Assignment 2 over the mode, though nine datasets cannot separate size
from the datasets themselves. We had proposed that MICA's partitions are too reproducible for
reproducibility to discriminate, because its distance between two cells does not change when the
sample does. That property is exact in both modes and may matter most when cells are few, but we
have not tested it in isolation. The $k$-means step that follows is not deterministic either: at
a fixed $k$ MICA's ARI moves by as much as 0.267 across seeds, on Buettner at $k=7$.

\subsection{What it changes for someone using these tools}
Within what we tested, a user should do five things differently. Compare methods on the same
cluster counts with one setting each, rather than only under the same cap, and check whether a
method's chosen count is one the other could have produced. On a dataset of a few hundred cells,
do not let a stability score choose MICA's number of clusters on its own, and if most settings
tie at the maximum or two readings of the same scores disagree, treat the rule as having made no
choice. Report the chosen cluster count beside the score, which exposes both failures at once.
Do not read the strength of MICA's default of four clusters as evidence that it generalises,
since tuning on this benchmark's labels picks the same value. And judge a faster neighbour graph
by the clustering it yields and by its memory, not by recall and time alone.

\subsection{Limitations}
The comparison covers one method against one baseline, under rules we chose, on datasets from a
single benchmark. The matched design was fixed before its runs but after the post hoc check
that prompted it, and one setting per count, the middle of those giving it, is one reasonable
choice among several. Even the matched grids reach 14 to 19 of the 19 counts per run, and MICA's
graph mode never makes fewer than 4 clusters on PBMC14K, so 2 and 3 clusters are never compared
there. The mode test runs at a single size on a single dataset. Some datasets carry labels derived by clustering the same
data, which flatters methods resembling the original pipeline, and PBMC14K is the only large
dataset in the selection comparison. We use 25 subsamples where chooseR specifies 100, which widens the bootstrap intervals
and lowers the threshold, and we report an observed range over three seeds rather than a
confidence interval, because three runs do not support one. Our implementation follows
chooseR's published description rather than running the authors' R code, and since the
threshold check shows the choice is sensitive at the level a bootstrap difference could
produce, a discrepancy in our reading would propagate to every stability result in the paper,
the matched comparison of Table~\ref{tab:matched} included. The graph comparison covers two datasets, lets the labels pick the
resolution so that only the graph differs, and compares numpy against compiled code, so it
bounds the cost of the two graphs on this machine rather than ranking the algorithms in
general.
