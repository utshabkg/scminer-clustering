\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{What the confirmatory runs settle.} The conclusions Assignment 3 drew about the rule held at
seven new seeds. The reversal on the original grids replicated, and on the nine small datasets it is our
sturdiest result: it holds at every seed, without any one dataset and at either number of subsamples. On PBMC14K the original grids favour MICA instead, \V{pbmc.S0.original.M.mica} against \V{pbmc.S0.original.M.leiden}, because there it is MICA's own grid that cannot reach 4 clusters. The reading still moved MICA more than the baselines, and MICA's flat top
stayed in the smallest datasets. The conclusion about the methods held only in part. MICA's lead on
matched candidates held under chooseR's reading at every new seed, under our scorer and under chooseR's
code with the declared repair, but dataset by dataset it splits about evenly, and with 25 subsamples it
rests on Buettner. Under the plain maximum the two methods are level with 25 subsamples, and MICA leads with 100, a comparison the protocol left without a verdict. So this benchmark says more about how the evaluation shapes a ranking than about which method
clusters better.

\textbf{Why the ranking moves.} Four mechanisms account for what we saw, and each was measured rather
than assumed. The first is the menu. A rule can only pick from what it is shown, and Leiden's original
grid never produced some of the counts the plain maximum prefers. PCA plus $k$-means shows the size of
this effect. It shares Leiden's representation, so the gap between those two reflects the menu and the
clustering step, and under the plain maximum that gap shrinks from \V{E2.original.M.leidenminuskmeans} on
the original grids to \V{E2.mid.M.leidenminuskmeans} once the counts are matched. It shares MICA's menu
and clustering step, so the gap between those two reflects the representation, and that gap barely
moves, \V{E2.original.M.micaminuskmeans} against \V{E2.mid.M.micaminuskmeans}. The candidates changed which method looked better, while the representation contributed about the same in both designs, though under the plain maximum that contribution rests on Usoskin.
Haslbeck and Wulff found that a stability estimate depends on its candidate range in the same way
\cite{Haslbeck2020Corrected}.

The second mechanism is ties. When many settings reproduce almost perfectly, stability no longer
separates them and the tie-break decides, toward fewer clusters under the plain maximum and toward more
under chooseR's reading. Ben-David et al. showed that stability alone cannot tell such settings apart
\cite{BenDavid2006Sober}, and here it is mostly MICA's problem. Its distance between two cells does not
change when the sample does, and on a few hundred cells removing a fifth of them often leaves its
partitions unchanged \cite{vonLuxburg2010Stability}, which may be why its scores are flat where Leiden's
are not.

The third is the interval. The threshold is the highest lower bound among the candidates, so how the
interval is computed decides which settings clear it. A coarse partition that reproduces perfectly is
where the two implementations part most. Under ours its lower bound is 1.0 and becomes the threshold, so only a finer setting that also reproduces perfectly clears it. Leiden on Klein had none. Under chooseR's code it gets no
interval at all, and a single such candidate leaves the threshold undefined, so the code as written makes
no choice in most MICA runs. Resampling clusters also gives wide intervals when there are only a few
clusters, and on PBMC14K that alone moved MICA's choice on its original grid from
\V{pbmc.S2.original.F.mica.k} clusters to \V{pbmc.S3G.original.F.mica.k}. The fourth mechanism is the
number of subsamples, which left the matched finest-stable comparison unchanged in direction but turned
the matched plain-maximum comparison from level into a MICA lead, and the original-grid finest-stable
comparison from level into a MICA lead as well.

\textbf{When a label-free comparison is reliable.} Within what we tested, six checks flag a ranking that should not be trusted, and none of them needs labels. No comparison here passes all six, so we have not shown that passing them is enough.
(1) The methods choose among the same cluster counts, and the ranking does not change with the setting behind each count. A shared cap is not enough, and that setting should be reported, since offering every setting per count instead of one lowered Leiden's
plain-maximum mean from \V{S0.mid.M.leiden} to \V{S0.dense.M.leiden} on the small datasets, and offering
only its lowest setting broke the tie on PBMC14K.
(2) The two readings of the rule rank the methods the same way.
(3) The scores are not flat. If most settings tie at the top, the tie-break is choosing.
(4) The ranking holds across subsample draws. Across seeds the draw moved Leiden's chosen ARI on one dataset by as much as \V{range.M.leiden.max} under the plain maximum.
(5) The ranking holds across implementations: the number of subsamples, the bootstrap and what it
resamples. The rule should also be able to form an interval at all.
(6) The ranking survives leaving out any one dataset.
Our matched finest-stable comparison passes checks (1) and (4). It passes check (5) only with the repair
the protocol declared, since chooseR's code as written makes no choice in most MICA runs, and it passes
check (6) only with 100 subsamples. It fails check (2) at 25 subsamples and check (3) on the five smallest
datasets, and even where it passes, the datasets split about evenly between the methods, so a mean lead
says little about the next dataset. We therefore read it as a lead for MICA under chooseR's reading on
these datasets, not as evidence that mutual information clusters better in general. The PBMC14K tie
fails check (1), since under the protocol's scorer it changes with the setting behind each count. A user choosing a method for new
data can run the same checks, and should report the chosen number of clusters beside any score, because
a respectable ARI can come with a badly wrong count.

\textbf{Limitations.} The rules all come from one family, subsampling stability, and the datasets from one
benchmark, nine of them small. Other label-free rules, such as significance tests, may rank the methods
differently. The matched design was introduced after we had seen the first results. Its conclusions are
now tested at new seeds under a protocol tagged in advance, but the criteria were written knowing the
results at seeds 0 to 2, and the per-dataset analysis was added afterwards. The protocol also left exploratory the effects of the number of subsamples, the interval and the setting per count, so these still need runs of their own. C4 counts PBMC14K seeds 0 to 2 as well, which we had seen in Assignment 3, and their three ties alone would satisfy it. Of the two new seeds, one ties and one gives MICA the lead. PBMC14K is the only large
dataset. MICA's graph mode never gives fewer than 4 clusters on it, so the matched comparison there starts
at 4, and its node2vec and Louvain take no seed, so its runs cannot be repeated exactly. The $k$-means
control isolates the representation only in MICA's small-data mode. The repairs for chooseR's failed
intervals are ours, though both were declared before the runs. The large-scale dataset the course
requires has not been run yet, and the secondary rules remain at three seeds or one.
