\begin{abstract}
Benchmarks for single-cell clustering usually fix each method's setting with the reference
labels, so their scores assume information a user does not have. We ask what happens to the
advantage scMINER reports for its mutual information clustering, MICA, over a principal
component and Leiden pipeline when one label-free rule chooses the setting for both. The rule
is subsampling stability in the style of chooseR, fixed before scoring and run at three seeds
on all ten benchmark datasets. As first specified, MICA leads under three of four rules on
the nine small datasets, narrowly under two of them, but its mean adjusted Rand index moves by 0.255 across rules
against 0.120 for the baseline, and two readings of the same stability scores reverse the
ranking. The reversal comes from the candidates: capping both methods at 20 clusters does not
give them the same cluster counts. Rerun with both offered the same counts, one setting each,
MICA leads under both readings at every seed, 0.662 against 0.543 and 0.473 against 0.452,
because the baseline falls once its grid reaches coarse partitions. MICA's own ambiguity
remains. Its stability scores are flat at the top, mostly on datasets of a few hundred cells,
and the tie-break still moves it more, 0.20 against 0.12 per dataset. Tuned on the other
datasets' labels, MICA picks its shipped default of four clusters in 26 of 27 cases. On PBMC14K, the one large
benchmark dataset, both methods choose at or near the coarsest count on offer, they tie under
the plain maximum, and a 0.02 change in the rule's threshold reverses which leads. We also
compare a faster
neighbour graph, a correlation shortlist rescored exactly, with MICA's own search. On PBMC14K
it matches MICA's recall and clustering in a small fraction of the build time. On 100,000
Allen cells it takes less than half the build time and reaches higher recall, but gives no better
clustering. On both it needs three to four times the memory.
\end{abstract}
