\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
We report what the rule chooses as first specified and on matched candidates, test why its readings
disagree, and finish with cost and the neighbour graph. Means cover the nine small datasets unless
stated. The starting point is sound: under the authors' own protocol, on their released inputs, MICA
reaches the published ARI within 0.05 on nine of ten datasets and within 0.001 on six, the exception
being Kolodziejczyk at 0.727, like the main run in the authors' notebook, against a published 1.000. With labels fixing the setting and 100 $k$-means restarts, the nine small datasets average 0.809 for MICA against 0.614 for the baseline.

\subsection{As first specified, a shared cap reverses the ranking}
MICA has the higher mean under three of the four rules (Table~\ref{tab:summary}, with per-dataset picks in Table~\ref{tab:rulesall}), but the rule moves its
score more than the choice of method does: from 0.473 to 0.727, a spread of 0.255, where the baseline's
spread is 0.120. The clearest case is the stability rule, whose two readings are
the same scores. MICA leads 0.661 to 0.632 under chooseR's reading, though only at two of
three seeds, and trails 0.473 to 0.635 under the plain maximum, so its mean moves by 0.189 between them
against the baseline's 0.002 (differences use unrounded means). The baseline's small figure is partly
cancellation, since in absolute terms its readings move 0.067 per dataset against 0.197 for MICA. 

That reversal comes from the candidates rather than the method. MICA's $k$ covers the whole range from 2 to
20, while the baseline's grid produces only some of it, for example 2 and 4 to 8 clusters on Yan and 4 to 11
on Pollen, and MICA's two readings land at counts the baseline cannot produce, 2 clusters on Pollen and 19 on
Yan. Restricting MICA's saved scores after the fact to the counts the baseline produced removes the reversal
(Appendix~\ref{app:common}), which is why we ran the matched design.

\subsection{On matched candidates the reversal is gone}\label{sec:matched}
Offered the same cluster counts with one setting each, MICA leads under both readings at every seed
(Table~\ref{tab:matched}), 0.662 to 0.543 under chooseR's reading and 0.473 to 0.452 under the plain maximum.
The AMI ranks them the same way, 0.775 against 0.646 and 0.602 against 0.585. The change is in the baseline,
not in MICA, whose means barely move. The baseline falls from 0.632 to 0.543 and from 0.635 to 0.452, and under
the plain maximum its median pick is now 2 or 3 clusters on eight of the nine datasets. The wider range, not the
single setting per count, causes the fall: in 13 of 27 runs the baseline's plain-maximum pick is a count its old
grid never produced, while keeping one setting per count on its old grid leaves its plain-maximum mean at 0.656
against 0.635. The silhouette rule on the same candidates, scored in the principal components, follows the same
pattern, with MICA at 0.593 and the baseline falling from 0.564 to 0.478, but in MICA's own embedding MICA trails,
0.493 against 0.497 (Appendix~\ref{app:secondary}).

Two qualifications keep this from being a clean win. The tie-break still moves MICA more, 0.197 per dataset in
absolute terms against 0.118, and the gap narrowed only because the baseline's figure rose from 0.067. MICA leads
on five of the nine datasets under each reading, so the mean rests on a few large gaps, such as Buettner at 0.934
against 0.263 and Pollen at 0.898 against 0.500. 

The chosen counts show why the scores alone understate the problem. Under chooseR's reading on matched candidates
MICA picks 19 or 20 clusters on Yan, which has 8 types, 14 to 18 on Pollen, which has 11, and 10 to 12 on Chung,
which has 4, and errs the other way on Zeisel with 3 where there are 7. The baseline can produce the same counts
here, so the overclustering is MICA's and not an artefact of the range.

\subsection{Why: the rule cannot separate the settings}
Figure~\ref{fig:curve} plots the quantity the rule maximises against the number of clusters. On Buettner MICA's
stability falls steadily as clusters are added, and the rule selects 3 clusters for an ARI of 0.934. On Pollen it
stays close to its maximum from 2 to 20 clusters, leaving the threshold nothing to act on. On Zeisel stability is
highest at 2 and 3 clusters while ARI peaks at 8, so the rule has a clear maximum in the wrong place.

Matching the candidates does not change this. With one setting per count for both, 40 percent of MICA's settings
score within 0.01 of its best against 14 percent for the baseline, the span of cluster counts among those tied
settings is 7.3 against 2.9, and 29 percent of MICA's settings score exactly 1.0 against 5 percent. Buettner aside,
the flat top sits in the smallest datasets. On the five with 124 to 317 cells the tied span is 10.6 for MICA against
4.2, and 81, 72 and 52 percent of MICA's settings score 1.0 on Yan, Pollen and Chung. On the four with 622 to 2,903
cells the spans are 3.3 against 1.3.

Two expected explanations fail on the original grids. Stability does not carry less information about accuracy for MICA: the
rank correlation between stability and ARI across settings averages 0.65 against 0.50 for the baseline. Nor is
overclustering worse where a method's spread of scores is narrower: the Spearman correlation is $-0.03$ for MICA and
$-0.21$ for the baseline.

Relaxing the threshold by at most 0.02,
a size we assumed rather than measured, changes the chosen cluster count in 35 of 54 runs on the original grids and
38 of 54 on matched candidates. Repeating the rule at three seeds moves the resulting ARI on matched candidates by
0.09 on average for MICA and 0.16 for the baseline, and by as much as 0.50, from 0.361 to 0.862 for the baseline on
Zeisel. The tie-break and the subsample draw each move ARI by about as much as the gap between the methods.

\subsection{PBMC14K: both choose at or near the coarsest count}\label{sec:pbmc}
PBMC14K has 13,605 cells, which puts it in graph mode, here with exact neighbours from one saved distance matrix in place of HNSW. On the original grids MICA scores 0.629 at 5 clusters
against the baseline's 0.397 at 4, but both are at the coarsest partition their grid allows. On matched candidates
both are offered 14 to 16 of the 17 counts from 4 to 20, and the plain maximum sends both to 4 clusters at every
seed, where they tie at 0.395 against 0.397, with AMI 0.720 against 0.725. chooseR's reading gives MICA a median of 0.623 at 4 or 5 clusters. Relaxing the threshold by 0.02 moves the baseline
to 6 or 7 clusters, at 0.786 to 0.875, ahead of MICA. So which method the rule favours here turns on its reading and
its threshold, and at the stated threshold neither pick is right: MICA's full sweep peaks at the true 7, at 0.856 to 0.864.

\label{sec:modesize}To separate MICA's pipeline from the dataset size, we also ran the rule on one fixed pool of 2,500
PBMC14K cells under both of MICA's modes, redrawing the pool at three seeds (Appendix~\ref{app:modesize}). On matched counts the readings differ on 2 of 3 pools in small-data mode against 1 of 3 in graph mode, too few to call a mode effect. Neither mode shows the flat top of the smallest
datasets at 2,500 cells, which fits the pattern above.

\subsection{The default and the transfer add no support}\label{sec:transfer}
MICA's best mean, 0.727, comes from its shipped default, which returns
four clusters whatever the dataset. The median number of cell types across these nine is also four, and the default
scores 0.802 where the true count is 3 to 5 against 0.579 outside. The transfer comparison, which uses the other datasets'
labels and is kept apart for that reason, shows why this is no support. Tuned on the other eight datasets' labels, MICA
takes $k = 4$ in 26 of 27 held-out choices, so its default is what tuning on this benchmark picks. The baseline's tuned
resolution, 0.22 to 0.30 against its shipped 1.0, lifts its mean from 0.514 to 0.641, still below MICA's 0.727.

\subsection{The cost of choosing, apart from clustering}
Choosing a setting costs far more than clustering at one, and a user pays both. On Zeisel one clustering at a fixed setting
takes a few seconds for either method once MICA's distance matrix exists, and that matrix takes 4 minutes and is computed
once. Across the nine small datasets selection takes 17 to 109 s for MICA and 14 to 225 s for the baseline on the original
grids, and a median of 25 s and 9 s per run on matched candidates, so cost is no reason to prefer either method there. On
PBMC14K one run of MICA as shipped at a fixed resolution takes under 12 minutes. The baseline's PCA, neighbour graph and sweep of 200
resolutions together took 2 to 3.3 minutes per seed in Assignment 2, and we saved no time for one clustering.
Choosing took 103 minutes per seed for MICA against 8 for the baseline on the original grids, and 93 against 7.5 on matched
candidates. MICA's selection times leave its distance matrix out, and on PBMC14K that matrix's compute time was not logged.
The matched times also leave out the full-data sweep that fixes the shared counts, at most 36 s per small-dataset run and about
16 minutes per PBMC14K seed. Graph-mode selection is why the large-dataset evidence rests on one dataset.

\subsection{The faster neighbour graph, on cost and quality}\label{sec:graph}
Table~\ref{tab:graph} compares MICA's HNSW search with our correlation shortlist rescored exactly. On PBMC14K a shortlist of 800
matches MICA's recall, 0.974 against 0.973, and ARI, 0.853 against 0.852, and builds the graph in 18 s against 7 minutes. The whole run is only about twice as fast, 6.4 minutes against
13.2, because node2vec and Louvain take about 6 minutes on either graph.

On the Allen cells a shortlist of 3,200 reaches higher recall than HNSW, 0.946 against 0.903, yet the clustering it yields is no
better: ARI 0.602 and 0.627 against 0.641 and 0.617. So recall alone does not settle which graph is better. The speed-up is also much
smaller here, 1.7 hours against 3.8 for the build and 2.8 against 4.9 in total. Build memory goes the other way on both datasets, 3.6 GB
against 1.2 on PBMC14K and 80 GB against 22 on Allen, with the peak reached while loading and binning the data. Appendix~\ref{app:cost} gives further
cautions on these times.
