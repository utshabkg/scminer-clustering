\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
We first confirm that the starting point is sound, then report what each label-free rule
chooses, then test why the rules disagree as much as they do.

\subsection{The reproduction holds}
On the authors' released inputs and under their own protocol, MICA reaches the published
ARI within 0.05 on nine of ten datasets and within 0.001 on six. The exception is
Kolodziejczyk, where the released files give 0.727 against a published 1.000, and the
authors' own notebook also gives 0.727, so the published value appears to come from an
input that was not released. These numbers were established in Assignment 2 and are
unchanged. They matter here only because they license what follows: when the labels fix the
setting, our MICA runs behave as the paper reports.

\subsection{MICA leads under most rules, but by amounts the rule decides}
Table~\ref{tab:rules} reports what each method achieves when the same rule chooses its
setting without labels. MICA has the higher mean under three of the four rules. That is the
first thing to say, and on its own it would read as support for the published claim.

The second thing is that the rules disagree with each other more than the methods disagree
under any one rule. MICA's mean ARI ranges from 0.473 to 0.727 depending only on which rule selects its
setting, a spread of 0.255. The baseline's ranges from 0.514 to 0.635, a spread of
0.120. Under the rule most favourable to MICA it leads by 0.213, and under the least
favourable it trails by 0.162. A study reporting any single one of these rules would be
making what looks like a statement about two clustering methods, when a large part of it is
a statement about the rule.

\subsection{Two readings of one rule reverse the ranking}
The clearest case is the stability rule, whose two columns are the same scores read two
ways. The published chooseR rule keeps the finest setting that clears a reproducibility
threshold. The second column keeps the highest-scoring setting outright. They share the
subsamples, the candidate settings and the scores, and differ only in how settings that
score alike are separated.

Under the published reading MICA leads, 0.661 against 0.632. Under the second it trails,
0.473 against 0.635. Between the two readings MICA's mean moves by 0.189 and the
baseline's by 0.003. The baseline is nearly indifferent to the tie-break and MICA is not.

Individual datasets show it more sharply. On Pollen MICA scores 0.896 under the published
reading and 0.087 under the other, because the first keeps 18 clusters and the second
collapses to 2. Neither is near the 11 types present. The baseline moves from 0.755 to
0.509 on the same dataset, a smaller swing from a smaller change in count.

\subsection{The chosen cluster counts}
Table~\ref{tab:clusters} shows why the scores alone understate the problem. Under the
published stability reading MICA chooses 19 clusters on Yan, which has 8 types, 18 on
Pollen, which has 11, and 12 on Chung, which has 4. It errs the other way on Zeisel,
choosing 3 where there are 7. A user inspecting those partitions would see the problem at
once, which is the argument for reporting the count beside the score.

\subsection{Why: the rule cannot separate the settings}
Figure~\ref{fig:curve} plots the quantity the rule maximises against the number of
clusters. On Buettner MICA's stability falls steadily as clusters are added, the rule has a
clear maximum near the true value, and it selects 3 clusters for an ARI of 0.934. On Pollen
MICA's stability stays close to its maximum across the whole range from 2 to 20 clusters,
leaving the threshold nothing to act on.

Measured across all datasets, 38 percent of MICA's candidate settings score within 0.01
of its best, against 22 percent for the baseline. The consequence is clearer in cluster
counts than in scores: among the settings that tie with the best, the span of cluster
counts averages 7.3 for MICA and 2.7 for the baseline. On Yan the rule cannot separate 3 clusters
from 19, and on Pollen it cannot separate 2 from 18.

One explanation we expected does not hold. We supposed stability would carry less
information about accuracy for MICA. It carries slightly more: the rank correlation between stability and ARI across settings
averages 0.65 for MICA against 0.50 for the baseline. The signal is not pointing the
wrong way, it is compressed into a band too narrow for a threshold to act on. We also
tested a prediction that followed from this, that the narrower a method's spread of
scores, the worse its overclustering should be. It is not supported. Across nine
datasets and three seeds the Spearman correlation is $-0.03$ for MICA and $-0.21$ for
the baseline, so if anything the association is weaker for the method whose scores are
more compressed. We report the prediction as failed. The direct measurement of ambiguity
above is an observation rather than an inference and does not rest on it.


A second check points the same way. The threshold the rule applies is the largest lower
bootstrap bound across its settings. Relaxing that threshold by at most 0.02 changes the
chosen cluster count in 14 of 18 dataset and method pairs, 8 of 9 for MICA and 6 of 9
for the baseline. That is the size of difference the choice of bootstrap could itself
produce, so the selection rests on a quantity our implementation can only estimate. We
had assumed this detail would not matter, and it does.

\subsection{The rule most favourable to MICA is the one tied to this benchmark}
MICA's best result comes from its own shipped default. Its small-data mode returns four
clusters when no setting is given, on any dataset of any size. That rule gives a mean ARI
of 0.727 and leads on seven of nine datasets.

The median number of cell types across these datasets is also four. Splitting the datasets
by that, MICA's default scores a mean ARI of 0.802 where the true count is between 3 and 5,
and 0.579 where it is outside. Three of the nine have exactly four types. We cannot show
how that default was chosen, and we make no claim about it. What we can say is that its
apparent strength is a property of this benchmark's composition rather than of the method,
and that it would not transfer to tissue with a different number of cell types. The
baseline's default is a resolution rather than a count, and it overclusters badly on these
small datasets, reaching 21 clusters on Zeisel.

\subsection{The silhouette rule depends on where it is computed}
A second incidental choice moves the result by a comparable amount. Scoring the silhouette
of the same partitions in the 50 principal components rather than in MICA's own embedding
changes the selected ARI by 0.13 on average for MICA and 0.16 for the baseline, reaching
0.39 on Goolam and 0.48 on Usoskin. Only the space changes. The partitions, the candidates
and the method are identical. Assignment 2 scored each method in its own space, and this is
why the rule is now computed in one shared space for both.


\subsection{The rule is also sensitive to the subsample draw} Repeating each rule with
three independent seeds, which redraw the subsamples and reinitialise the clustering,
moves the resulting ARI by 0.09 on average for both methods, and by as much as 0.46. The
largest swings are MICA on Buettner under the second reading, from 0.474 to 0.934, and
MICA on Klein under the published reading, from 0.501 to 0.863. The seed is not a
property of either method or of the data. Taken with the threshold check above, three
incidental choices inside one selection rule, the tie-break, the threshold and the
random draw, each move the result by an amount comparable to the gap between the two
methods.

\subsection{Cost}
The stability rule costs 494 clusterings per dataset for MICA over 19 candidate values of
$k$, and about 1,040 for the baseline over its resolution grid, in both cases 25 subsample
reruns plus the full-data fit. On these datasets that is 15 to 106 seconds for MICA and 14 to 211 seconds for the
baseline on 8 threads, excluding the mutual information distance
matrix, which is computed once per dataset and shared by every setting and seed.

That one-off cost is the larger number and falls only on MICA: 6 to 11 minutes on the two
datasets above 2,500 cells, against seconds on the smallest. It is also what makes the rule
affordable. Because MICA's distance between two cells does not depend on any other cell, the
matrix is computed once and subsampled exactly rather than recomputed 25 times. Without
that property the rule would cost roughly 25 times more for MICA, and the comparison in
Table~\ref{tab:rules} would not have been feasible on this hardware.
