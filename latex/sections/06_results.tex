\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
We first confirm that the starting point is sound, then report what each label-free rule
chooses, then test why the rules disagree as much as they do.

\subsection{The reproduction holds}
Under the authors' own protocol, on their released inputs, MICA reaches the published ARI
within 0.05 on nine of ten datasets and within 0.001 on six, the exception being
Kolodziejczyk at 0.727 against a published 1.000, as is the authors' own notebook. These
numbers are unchanged from Assignment 2 and license what follows: when the labels fix the
setting, our MICA runs behave as the paper reports.

\subsection{MICA leads under most rules, but by amounts the rule decides}
Table~\ref{tab:rulesall} in the appendix reports what each method achieves when the same
rule chooses its setting without labels. MICA has the higher mean under three of the four
rules, so a study that reported any one of them would most likely report an advantage. But
for MICA the choice of rule moves the score more than the choice of method does: its mean
ARI ranges from 0.473 to 0.727 depending only on which rule selects its setting, a spread of
0.255, where the baseline's spread is 0.120. Under the rule most favourable to MICA it leads
by 0.213, and under the least favourable, the plain maximum, it trails by 0.162. That last gap
comes from the range of candidates, as the next two subsections show. The other two leads are
small, 0.031 under the silhouette rule and 0.029 under chooseR's reading, and the second is not
stable: MICA leads at two of the three seeds and trails at the third, 0.639 against 0.671. Differences here are computed from unrounded means, so they can
differ in the last digit from the rounded figures.

\subsection{Two readings of one rule move MICA more than the baseline}
The clearest case is the stability rule, whose two columns in Table~\ref{tab:rules} are the
same scores read two ways: chooseR keeps the finest setting clearing a reproducibility
threshold, the second keeps the highest-scoring setting outright. They share the subsamples,
the candidates and the scores. On each method's own grid MICA leads 0.661 to 0.632 under the
published reading and trails 0.473 to 0.635 under the other, so its mean moves by 0.189
between them against the baseline's 0.002. The baseline's small figure is partly cancellation:
its readings differ on five of the nine datasets, in both directions, and in absolute terms
they move 0.067 per dataset against 0.197 for MICA. On Pollen MICA scores 0.898 at 17 clusters
under the first reading and 0.087 at 2 under the second, while the baseline stays at 0.755 and
7 clusters.

That reversal comes from the candidates rather than the method. The 2 to 20 filter caps both
grids but does not match them. MICA's $k$ covers the whole range on every dataset, while the
baseline's resolution grid produces only some of it, for example 2 and 4 to 8 clusters on Yan
and 4 to 11 on Pollen, and MICA's two readings land at counts the baseline cannot produce, 2
clusters on Pollen and 19 on Yan. Restricting MICA's saved scores after the fact to the counts
the baseline produced removes the reversal (Appendix~\ref{app:common}), which is why we ran
the matched design of Section~\ref{sec:matcheddesign}.

\subsection{On matched candidates the reversal is gone}\label{sec:matched}
Offered the same cluster counts with one setting each, MICA leads under both readings at every
seed (Table~\ref{tab:matched}), 0.662 to 0.543 under chooseR's reading and 0.473 to 0.452 under
the plain maximum. The AMI ranks them the same way, 0.775 against 0.646 and 0.602 against
0.585. Each small-dataset run offered 14 to 19 of the 19 counts. The change is in the baseline,
not in MICA. MICA's means barely move from its own grid, 0.661 to 0.662 and 0.473 to 0.473, and
where all 19 counts were offered its picks are the same as before in 8 of 8 cases. The baseline
falls from 0.632 to 0.543 and from 0.635 to 0.452 on the wider grid, and under the plain maximum
its median pick is now 2 or 3 clusters on eight of the nine datasets. The wider range, not the
single setting per count, causes the fall: in 13 of 27 runs the baseline's plain-maximum pick is
a count its old grid never produced, while keeping one setting per count on its old grid leaves
its plain-maximum mean at 0.656 against 0.635. Its old grid had kept it away from the coarse
partitions the plain maximum favours.

Three qualifications keep this from being a clean win. The tie-break still moves MICA more,
0.197 per dataset in absolute terms against 0.118. That is the same 0.197 as on the original
grids, so the gap narrowed only because the baseline's figure rose from 0.067. MICA leads on five of the nine datasets under each reading, so the mean
rests on a few large gaps, such as Buettner at 0.934 against 0.263 and Pollen at 0.898 against
0.500. And the matched design was chosen after the post hoc check. That check had suggested a
larger lead, 0.772 under chooseR's reading, because it held MICA to the baseline's narrow range,
on Yan 2 and 4 to 8 clusters, where MICA's finest-stable pick fell from 19 to 7. Offered the full range
again, MICA picks 19 or 20 clusters on Yan.

\subsection{The chosen cluster counts} The counts in Table~\ref{tab:rules} show why the
scores alone understate the problem. Under the published stability reading MICA chooses 19
clusters on Yan, which has 8 types, 17 on Pollen, which has 11, and 11 on Chung, which has
4, and errs the other way on Zeisel with 3 where there are 7. The counts also move with the
seed, by 3 clusters on Pollen and 1 on Yan, so the overclustering is not a single bad draw.
On matched candidates, where the baseline can produce the same counts, MICA still picks 19 or
20 clusters on Yan, 14 to 18 on Pollen and 10 to 12 on Chung, so the overclustering is MICA's
and not an artefact of the range. A user would see all of this at once, which is the argument
for reporting the count beside the score.

\subsection{Why: the rule cannot separate the settings}
Figure~\ref{fig:curve} plots the quantity the rule maximises against the number of
clusters. On Buettner MICA's stability falls steadily as clusters are added, the rule has a
clear maximum near the true value, and it selects 3 clusters for an ARI of 0.934. On Pollen
MICA's stability stays close to its maximum across the whole range from 2 to 20 clusters,
leaving the threshold nothing to act on. Zeisel shows the third pattern: stability is at
its highest for 2 and 3 clusters while ARI peaks at 8, so the rule has a clear maximum and
it is in the wrong place.

Across the nine small datasets, 38 percent of MICA's candidate settings score within 0.01 of
its best, against 22 percent for the baseline. Among the settings that tie with the best, the
span of cluster counts averages 7.3 for MICA and 2.7 for the baseline. Matching the candidates
does not change this. With one setting per count for both, 40 percent of MICA's settings tie
against 14 percent, over a span of 7.3 against 2.9, and 29 percent of MICA's score exactly 1.0
against 5 percent. Buettner aside, the flat top sits in the smallest datasets. On the five with 124 to 317
cells the tied span is 10.6 for MICA against 4.2, and 81, 72 and 52 percent of MICA's settings
score 1.0 on Yan, Pollen and Chung. On the four with 622 to 2,903 cells the spans are 3.3
against 1.3, and at most 16 percent of MICA's settings score 1.0.

Two explanations we expected do not hold. Stability does not carry less information about
accuracy for MICA: the rank correlation between stability and ARI across settings averages
0.65 for MICA against 0.50 for the baseline, so the signal points the right way and only sits
in a band too narrow for a threshold to act on. Nor is overclustering worse where a method's
spread of scores is narrower: across nine datasets and three seeds the Spearman correlation
is $-0.03$ for MICA and $-0.21$ for the baseline. We report both as failed. The ambiguity
measured above is an observation and does not rest on either.

The threshold check points the same way. The threshold is the largest lower bootstrap bound
across settings, and relaxing it by at most 0.02 changes the chosen cluster count in 35 of 54
runs, 20 of 27 for MICA and 15 of 27 for the baseline, and in 38 of 54 on matched candidates. That is the size of
difference the choice of bootstrap could itself produce. We had assumed this detail would not
matter. It does.

\subsection{On the one large benchmark dataset, both methods choose at or near the coarsest count on offer}\label{sec:pbmc}
Everything above comes from MICA's small-data mode. PBMC14K has 13,605 cells, which puts it
in graph mode, and its labels are cell types fixed by antibody sorting rather than by
clustering. On the original grids both readings select 5 clusters for MICA at all three seeds,
and MICA scores 0.629 against the baseline's 0.397, with AMI 0.790 against 0.725.

That lead is weaker than it looks. Both methods pick the coarsest partition their grid allows:
MICA at resolution 4.0 or 3.9, the top of its grid, since in MICA's Louvain a higher resolution
gives fewer clusters, and the baseline at 0.05, the bottom of its own, at 4 clusters. On matched
candidates both are offered 14 to 16 of the 17 counts from 4 to 20, none below 4 since MICA's
graph mode makes no fewer than 4 clusters here, and the plain maximum sends both to 4 clusters
at every seed, where they tie at 0.395 against 0.397, with AMI 0.720 against 0.725. chooseR's reading keeps MICA at 5 clusters on two seeds, for a median of 0.623,
and moves it to 4 on the third. The baseline's best setting scores 0.876 to 0.878 and its
shipped default 0.853 to 0.867, above any pick here, and MICA's own graph-mode default scores
0.846. On either grid, relaxing the threshold by 0.02 moves the baseline to 6 or 7 clusters at
0.785 to 0.875, ahead of MICA. So which method the rule favours here turns on its reading and its
threshold, and neither pick is right: MICA's ARI peaks at the true 7 clusters, at 0.851 to
0.859 on the original grid and 0.856 to 0.864 on the matched runs' longer one.

\subsection{Mode or size: the same cells, run both ways}\label{sec:modesize}
To separate MICA's pipeline from the dataset size, we drew a fixed pool of 2,500 PBMC14K cells
and ran the rule on those same cells under both of MICA's modes, at three seeds that redraw the
pool (Table~\ref{tab:modesize}, Appendix~\ref{app:modesize}). On the original grids this
suggests a mode effect: in small-data mode the two readings choose differently on every pool,
by a median of 0.319 ARI, and in graph mode on none. But graph mode makes few coarse
partitions. On MICA's own grid, up to a resolution of 4.0, it made no fewer than 4 or 5
clusters on these pools. Extending the resolution to 30 reaches 3 clusters on two pools and 2 on
one, while small-data mode's $k$ reaches 2 on every pool. On matched candidates, the
counts all three arms reach, the difference shrinks but does not vanish. The two readings now
differ on 2 of 3 pools in small-data mode, by a median of 0.185 ARI, against 1 of 3 in graph
mode and for the baseline. The tied counts span 3, 1 and 3 in small-data mode against 1, 1 and 1
in graph mode and 1, 1 and 2 for the baseline, and every arm picks 3 to 5 clusters where there
are 7 types. With three pools the test leaves at most a small effect of the mode, and since the
two modes also normalise differently it says nothing about the mutual information distance
itself. Neither mode shows the flat top of the
smallest datasets at 2,500 cells, which fits the pattern above.

\subsection{The default and silhouette rules favour MICA for reasons of their own}
MICA's best mean, 0.727, comes from its own shipped default, which returns four clusters
whatever the dataset. The median number of cell types across these nine is also four, and
the default scores 0.802 where the true count is 3 to 5 against 0.579 outside, so its
strength reflects this benchmark's composition rather than the method.
Appendix~\ref{app:secondary} gives that analysis and the silhouette rule's dependence on
the space it scores in.

\subsection{A setting tuned on the other datasets, which uses their labels}\label{sec:transfer}
The transfer comparison asks how far a setting tuned on labelled benchmarks carries to a new
one, so it is not label-free and we keep it apart (Table~\ref{tab:transfer},
Appendix~\ref{app:transfer}). Tuned on the other eight datasets' labels, MICA takes $k = 4$,
its shipped default, in 26 of 27 held-out choices, and $k = 3$ once. Its default is therefore
what tuning on this benchmark's labels picks, so its strong score here is the score of a setting
fitted to these datasets. That matches the composition explanation above and is not evidence
that four clusters carry over to other tissue. The baseline's tuned resolution, 0.22 to 0.30 against its shipped 1.0, lifts its
mean from 0.514 to 0.641, and MICA's is 0.727, so MICA still leads when both are tuned the same
way. On PBMC14K the baseline's transferred resolution gives 6 clusters at 0.786, well above
either method's label-free pick, and MICA's graph mode has no setting to transfer.

\subsection{Every rule in one table}
Table~\ref{tab:summary} gathers each rule's mean for both methods. MICA has the higher mean
under every rule except the plain maximum on the original grids, by margins from 0.020 to
0.213, and its own mean moves by 0.255 across the label-free rules. The silhouette rule on
matched candidates follows the stability rule: MICA's mean holds at 0.593 while the baseline's
falls from 0.564 to 0.478, as it now picks 2 clusters on seven of the nine datasets. We set no
win criterion in advance, because the point of the table is how far each method's score moves
with the rule.

\subsection{The rule is also sensitive to the subsample draw} Repeating the rule at three
independent seeds, which redraw the subsamples and reinitialise the clustering, moves the
resulting ARI by 0.09 on average for both methods and by as much as 0.46, from 0.474 to
0.934 for MICA on Buettner. The tie-break and the subsample draw each move ARI by about as
much as the gap between the methods, and the threshold moves the chosen count in 35 of 54
runs.

\subsection{The cost of choosing, apart from the cost of clustering}
Choosing a setting costs far more than clustering at one, and a user pays both. On Zeisel one
clustering at a fixed setting takes a few seconds for either method once MICA's distance
matrix exists, and that matrix takes 4 minutes and is computed once. Choosing the setting by
stability then adds 103 s for MICA and 107 s for the baseline. On PBMC14K one run of MICA as
shipped at a fixed resolution takes about 11 minutes, almost all of it the HNSW search and
node2vec. Choosing the resolution took 103 minutes per seed for MICA against 8 for the
baseline, even though our runner skips HNSW and takes exact neighbours for every subsample
from one full distance matrix computed once. Across the nine small datasets selection takes
17 to 109 s for MICA and 14 to 225 s for the baseline, on 494 clusterings for MICA and 468 to
1,040 for the baseline, and MICA's distance matrix, 4 to 6 minutes on the two datasets above
2,500 cells, is shared across every setting and seed. Without the exactness property it would
be rebuilt for each of the 25 subsamples. On matched candidates, with one setting per count,
selection takes a median of 24 s for MICA and 9 s for the baseline per small-dataset run, and 93
and 7.5 minutes per seed on PBMC14K. These matched times leave out the full-data sweep that
fixes the shared counts, which the log timestamps put, with loading, at 10 to 36 s per
small-dataset run and about 16 minutes per PBMC14K seed. Cost is no reason to prefer either method at this
size, but it bounds the study: graph-mode selection is why the large-dataset evidence rests on
one dataset.

\subsection{The faster neighbour graph, on cost and on the clustering it yields}\label{sec:graph}
Table~\ref{tab:graph} compares MICA's HNSW search with our correlation shortlist rescored
exactly, on PBMC14K and on 100,000 Allen cells.

On PBMC14K a shortlist of 800 matches MICA's recall, 0.974 against 0.973, and builds the graph
in 18 s against 7 minutes, of which generating the shortlist takes 4 s. Through MICA's own
node2vec and Louvain the two graphs give ARI 0.853 and 0.852 in each of two runs. The whole run
is only about twice as fast, 6.4 minutes against 13.2, because node2vec and Louvain take about
6 minutes on either graph. Memory goes the other way. The shortlist build peaks at
3.6 GB against 1.2 GB for HNSW, almost all of it spent loading and binning the data before the
search begins.

On the Allen cells a shortlist of 3,200 reaches higher recall than HNSW, 0.946 against 0.903,
yet the clustering it yields is no better: ARI 0.602 and 0.627 against 0.641 and 0.617, and
AMI 0.819 and 0.825 against 0.835 and 0.831. So recall alone does not settle which graph is
better, and we report both. The speed-up is also much smaller here, though the time saved is
larger. The shortlist builds this graph in 1.7 hours against 3.8, 2.2 times as fast where it
was 24 times as fast on PBMC14K. Adding MICA's node2vec and Louvain, timed in the Assignment 2
runs, brings the totals to 2.8 hours against 4.9. The memory gap widens to 80 GB against
22 GB, and again the peak is reached while loading and binning the data.

Three cautions apply to the times. Our build is faster than the 2.4 minutes we reported in
Assignment 2 because rescoring now uses an exact distance that skips genes where a cell sits
in its lowest bin. We checked it by building the PBMC14K graph both ways: the neighbour lists
are identical for every cell, and the dense path takes 87 s for the search alone. Our
rescoring is also numpy while MICA's search is compiled, both on 8 workers. And the build
times in Table~\ref{tab:graph} were measured on the same day on the 120-core machine, with at
most two builds running at once, always one MICA search beside one shortlist build, while the
node2vec and Louvain times inside the Total column come from the Assignment 2 runs. Counting
the search alone, every build ran faster in these runs than in Assignment 2: MICA's 2.1 times
on PBMC14K and 1.4 times on Allen, our dense path 1.7 times and our Allen build 1.3 times. We
therefore attribute part of the old timings to a busier machine.
