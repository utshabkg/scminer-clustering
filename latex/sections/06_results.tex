\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
We first confirm that the starting point is sound, then report what each label-free rule
chooses, then test why the rules disagree as much as they do.

\subsection{The reproduction holds}
Under the authors' own protocol, on their released inputs, MICA reaches the published ARI
within 0.05 on nine of ten datasets and within 0.001 on six, the exception being
Kolodziejczyk at 0.727 against a published 1.000, as is the authors' own notebook. These
numbers are unchanged from Assignment 2 and license what follows: when the labels fix the
setting, our MICA runs behave as the paper reports.

\subsection{MICA leads under most rules, but by amounts the rule decides}
Table~\ref{tab:rulesall} in the appendix reports what each method achieves when the same
rule chooses its setting without labels. MICA has the higher mean under three of the four
rules, so a study that reported any one of them would most likely report an advantage. But
for MICA the choice of rule
moves the score more than the choice of method does: its mean ARI ranges from 0.473 to
0.727 depending only on which rule selects its setting, a spread of 0.255, where the
baseline's spread is 0.120. Under the rule most favourable to MICA it leads by 0.213, and
under the least favourable it trails by 0.162.

\subsection{Two readings of one rule reverse the ranking}
The clearest case is the stability rule, whose two columns are the same scores read two
ways: chooseR keeps the finest setting clearing a reproducibility threshold, the second
keeps the highest-scoring setting outright. They share the subsamples, the candidates and
the scores, and differ only in how settings that score alike are separated. Under the
published reading MICA leads 0.661 to 0.632, under the second it trails 0.473 to 0.635, so
its mean moves by 0.189 between them against the baseline's 0.003.

Individual datasets show it more sharply. On Pollen MICA scores 0.898 under the published
reading and 0.087 under the other, keeping 17 clusters and then collapsing to 2, neither
near the 11 types present. The baseline's median is unchanged there, 0.755 at 7 clusters
under both readings.



\subsection{The chosen cluster counts} The counts in Table~\ref{tab:rules} show why the
scores alone understate the problem. Under the published stability reading MICA chooses 19
clusters on Yan, which has 8 types, 17 on Pollen, which has 11, and 11 on Chung, which has
4, and errs the other way on Zeisel with 3 where there are 7. The counts also move with
the seed, by 3 clusters on Pollen and 2 on Yan, so the overclustering is not a single bad
draw. A user would see all of this at once, which is the argument for reporting the count
beside the score.

\subsection{Why: the rule cannot separate the settings}
Figure~\ref{fig:curve} plots the quantity the rule maximises against the number of
clusters. On Buettner MICA's stability falls steadily as clusters are added, the rule has a
clear maximum near the true value, and it selects 3 clusters for an ARI of 0.934. On Pollen
MICA's stability stays close to its maximum across the whole range from 2 to 20 clusters,
leaving the threshold nothing to act on. Zeisel shows the third pattern: stability is at
its highest for 2 and 3 clusters while ARI peaks at 8, so the rule has a clear maximum and
it is in the wrong place.

Measured across all datasets, 38 percent of MICA's candidate settings score within 0.01
of its best, against 22 percent for the baseline. The consequence is clearer in cluster
counts than in scores: among the settings that tie with the best, the span of cluster
counts averages 7.3 for MICA and 2.7 for the baseline. On Yan and on Pollen the rule
cannot separate 2 clusters from 20, the full width of the candidate range.

One explanation we expected does not hold. We supposed stability would carry less
information about accuracy for MICA. It carries slightly more, the rank correlation
between stability and ARI across settings averaging 0.65 for MICA against 0.50 for the
baseline. The signal points the right way. It just sits in a band too narrow for a
threshold to act on. A prediction that followed from this, that the narrower a method's
spread of scores the worse its overclustering should be, is also unsupported: across nine
datasets and three seeds the Spearman correlation is $-0.03$ for MICA and $-0.21$ for the
baseline, so if anything the association runs the other way. We report it as failed. The
ambiguity measured above is an observation rather than an inference and does not rest on
it.


A second check points the same way. The threshold the rule applies is the largest lower
bootstrap bound across its settings. Relaxing that threshold by at most 0.02 changes the
chosen cluster count in 14 of 18 dataset and method pairs, 8 of 9 for MICA and 6 of 9
for the baseline. That is the size of difference the choice of bootstrap could itself
produce, so the selection rests on a quantity our implementation can only estimate. We had
assumed this detail would not matter. It does.

\subsection{On the one large dataset the ambiguity disappears}\label{sec:pbmc}
Everything above comes from MICA's small-data mode. PBMC14K has 13,605 cells, which puts
it in graph mode, and it is also the only dataset whose labels were fixed by antibody
sorting rather than by clustering. On it the ambiguity is absent, although the dataset
alone cannot say whether that is down to its size or to the pipeline, which
Section~\ref{sec:modesize} settles.

The settings that tie with MICA's best all return exactly 5 clusters, on
all three seeds, so the span that averages 7.3 on the small datasets is 0 here. Both readings of
the rule select the same setting, and the tie-break that reverses the ranking everywhere
else has nothing left to decide. MICA leads the baseline 0.629 to 0.397 in ARI and 0.790 to
0.725 in AMI. On the one dataset where the rule is unambiguous, and the one whose labels owe
nothing to a clustering algorithm, the published advantage survives label-free selection.

The rule still fails here, but differently and for both methods. MICA's stability rises as
the partition coarsens and peaks at the 5 clusters it selects, while its ARI peaks at the
true 7, at 0.851 to 0.859 across the three seeds. The baseline selects 4 where its best setting
would have given about 0.88. Both underclustered, and neither was ambiguous. The rule does
not fail in one way: on small data it cannot separate the settings, and here it separates
them confidently and picks the wrong one.

The threshold check agrees. Relaxing the threshold by up to 0.02 leaves MICA's chosen
count here unchanged, one of only five cases in twenty where it does not move, while the
baseline's shifts from 4 clusters to 6. One caution: this is a single dataset, and although
its three seeds agree to within 0.003 what replicates is the rule's behaviour, not the
biology.

\subsection{Mode or size: the same cells, run both ways}\label{sec:modesize}
PBMC14K differs from the nine small datasets in two ways at once, its size and MICA's
pipeline, so it cannot on its own say which removes the ambiguity. We therefore drew a
fixed pool of 2,500 PBMC14K cells and ran the same rule on those same cells under both of
MICA's modes, repeating it at three seeds that redraw the pool as well as the subsamples.
Within a seed the data, the size, the subsamples and the labels are identical across the
arms. Mode here means the whole path scMINER takes, since its two modes normalise the
mutual information differently as well as embedding it differently.

\input{tables/a3_mode_size}

The answer is the mode, and it does not depend on which cells we drew.
Table~\ref{tab:modesize} gives the three arms. In small-data mode the settings that tie
with the best span 4 cluster counts at the median and never fewer than 2, and the two
readings of the rule choose differently on every one of the three pools, by a median of
0.319 ARI. In graph mode on the same cells the median span is 0 and the two readings never
disagree. The baseline sits between them, with a span of 1 on every pool and one
disagreement in three. Graph mode is unambiguous at 2,500 cells and at 13,605 alike, so
size is not what sets Section~\ref{sec:pbmc} apart.

So the ambiguity is neither a peculiarity of the benchmark's datasets, since it appears on
PBMC14K once PBMC14K is cut to their size, nor a property of mutual information clustering,
since the same distance on the same cells produces none of it once MICA embeds that
distance as a graph rather than by multidimensional scaling. What the rest of this paper
measures is MICA's small-data mode, the mode nine of the ten benchmark datasets run in.

\subsection{The two secondary rules favour MICA for reasons of their own}
MICA's best mean, 0.727, comes from its own shipped default, which returns four clusters
whatever the dataset. The median number of cell types across these nine is also four, and
the default scores 0.802 where the true count is 3 to 5 against 0.579 outside, so its
strength reflects this benchmark's composition rather than the method.
Appendix~\ref{app:secondary} gives that analysis and the silhouette rule's dependence on
the space it scores in.

\subsection{The rule is also sensitive to the subsample draw} Repeating the rule at three
independent seeds, which redraw the subsamples and reinitialise the clustering, moves the
resulting ARI by 0.09 on average for both methods and by as much as 0.46, from 0.474 to
0.934 for MICA on Buettner. With the tie-break and the threshold, that makes three
incidental choices inside one rule, each moving the result by about as much as the gap
between the methods.

\subsection{Cost} The stability rule costs 494 clusterings per dataset for MICA and 468 to
1,040 for the baseline, which is 17 to 109 seconds and 14 to 225 seconds respectively on 8
threads, over the nine small-data datasets and three seeds. MICA also needs one distance
matrix per dataset, 6 to 11 minutes on the two above 2,500 cells, shared across every
setting and seed, which without the exactness property would be rebuilt 25 times. Cost is
no reason to prefer either method at this size, but it bounds the study: the graph-mode run
on PBMC14K took 103 minutes for MICA against 9 for the baseline, which is why the
large-dataset evidence rests on one dataset.
