\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
A biologist who has sequenced a new tissue wants to know which cell types are in it. The
standard route is to cluster the cells and inspect the groups. Every clustering method used
for this has a setting that controls how finely it divides the data, and the result depends
on it strongly. On Zeisel, which has 7 annotated cell types, the baseline returns 3 or 4
clusters at a resolution of 0.05, depending on the seed, and 21 at 1.0, the setting its
software ships with. Choosing it is therefore not a
detail of tuning but the main decision, and it has to be made without knowing the answer,
because the number of cell types is usually what the experiment is trying to establish.

Published benchmarks do not make that decision. In the benchmark for scMINER
\cite{pan2025scminer}, the framework we study, every method is either given the true
number of cell types or has its setting swept and keeps only the runs reproducing that
number, with ties broken by agreement with the labels. The reported scores are
conditional on information a user does not have. This is not specific to scMINER:
Ullmann et al. \cite{ullmann2023overoptimistic} show that tuning against the ground
truth inflates a method's apparent advantage, and a recent survey
\cite{szmigiel2025benchmarking} reports that on six of sixteen datasets the common
default does no better than a random setting. What is missing is a direct measurement of
what happens to a specific published advantage when the selection is made without
labels.

\textbf{Main question.} When the same label-free rule chooses the setting for both methods,
does MICA, the mutual information clustering in scMINER, produce partitions closer to the
known cell types than a principal component and Leiden pipeline on the same genes?

Two supporting questions follow from it. How much of the answer is set by the rule rather
than by either method, and under what conditions does the answer hold?

Our approach holds the data and the rule fixed and varies the method. Both methods receive
the same matrix and are selected by the same rule. As first specified, both chose among
settings yielding at most 20 clusters. In the runs we rely on, both are offered the same
cluster counts, those from 2 to 20 that every method reaches on the full data, with one setting
per count. The main rule is a published one, subsampling stability in the style of chooseR
\cite{pattersoncross2021chooser}, which reruns each method on random 80 percent
subsamples per candidate setting and prefers settings whose clusters survive resampling.
We use 25 subsamples where chooseR specifies 100, a reduction we justify and test in
Section~\ref{sec:design}. Labels are used only afterwards, to score what the rule chose.
The experiment covers all ten benchmark datasets at three seeds, with cost recorded.

The result is not the one we expected. MICA leads under three of the four rules, which on its
own would read as support for the published claim, but its mean moves by 0.255 across them
against 0.120 for the baseline, and two readings of one rule reverse the ranking. Checking why
exposed a confound: capping both methods at 20 clusters does not give them the same
candidates. Rerun on matched counts, the reversal is gone and MICA leads under both readings
at every seed, because the baseline's score falls once its grid reaches the coarse partitions
MICA's always could. MICA's own ambiguity does not go away. Its
stability scores stay flat at the top, mostly on the smallest datasets, and a label-informed
transfer shows that its default of four clusters is what tuning on this benchmark would pick.
On PBMC14K, the one large benchmark dataset, both methods choose at or near the coarsest
partition on offer, and the ranking turns on the rule's reading and threshold. Our first two
explanations for the ambiguity also failed their tests.

This submission makes three contributions.

\textbf{A measurement of what label-free selection costs, and of how unstable that cost
is.} The closest work establishes that benchmarks tuned on labels are optimistic
\cite{ullmann2023overoptimistic} and supplies rules meant to replace that tuning
\cite{pattersoncross2021chooser,liu2021multik}, but none has been applied to mutual
information clustering, and the scMINER benchmark reports no label-free result at all. We
supply that measurement, and find that the tie-break and the random subsample draw inside a
single rule each move the result by as much as the gap between the two methods, and that a
small change in its threshold changes the chosen cluster count in most cases. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} turns this into what a user choosing a method for
unlabelled data should do differently.

\textbf{Evidence that a cap is not a match, and of what remains once the candidates are
matched.} The reversal came from candidate counts one method could reach and the other could
not, and it disappears when both are offered the same counts, one setting each. What remains
is MICA's: its stability scores are flat at the top, mostly on datasets of a few hundred cells,
and the rule's two readings break that tie in opposite directions. We also prove and test an
exact identity, that MICA's distance on a subset of cells equals the
corresponding submatrix of the full one, which makes the experiment affordable.

\textbf{A cost and quality comparison of a faster neighbour graph.} A correlation shortlist
rescored with the exact MICA distance builds PBMC14K's graph in seconds rather than minutes
with the same recall and clustering, and the graph of 100,000 Allen cells in less than half the
time. It is not guaranteed to find every true neighbour, it needs three to four times the
memory, and on the larger dataset higher recall gave no better clustering, so it is a faster
graph rather than a better one.
