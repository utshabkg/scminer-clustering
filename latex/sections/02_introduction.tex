\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
A biologist who has sequenced a new tissue wants to know which cell types are in it. The standard
route is to cluster the cells and inspect the groups, but every practical method has a setting that
controls how finely it divides the data. On Zeisel, which has 7 annotated cell types, a Leiden
pipeline returns 3 or 4 clusters at resolution 0.05, depending on the seed, and 20 or 21 at resolution
1.0, the setting Scanpy ships with. A user has to make that choice without knowing the answer,
because the number of cell types is usually what the experiment is trying to establish.

Benchmarks rarely face that choice. scMINER \cite{pan2025scminer} reports that its mutual information
clustering module, MICA, beats Seurat and Scanpy on average over ten labelled datasets, but MICA is run
at the true number of clusters and the graph methods are swept until they reproduce that count. Tuning
against the labels can make a method look better than it would to a user who does not have them
\cite{ullmann2023overoptimistic}. In single-cell benchmarks the way settings are chosen can reorder the
methods \cite{raimundo2020tuning}, and the cluster count a setting gives can matter more than the method
itself \cite{germain2020pipecomp}. A fair comparison therefore lets each method choose its own setting
without labels. That opens a second problem, and it is the subject of this paper: the label-free rule,
and the candidates it is shown, can decide the ranking too.

\textbf{Main question.} How do the candidate settings and the label-free rule that chooses among them
affect the reliability of a comparison of clustering methods? We study it on MICA against two pipelines on
the same genes: principal component analysis (PCA) followed by Leiden, the usual single-cell route, and
PCA followed by $k$-means, the textbook baseline. Three questions follow. Why does offering every method
the same cluster counts change the ranking? Which details of the rule, its reading, its number of
subsamples and its interval, move the ranking? And what can a user check, with or without labels, before
trusting a ranking?

The rule is subsampling stability in the style of chooseR \cite{pattersoncross2021chooser}. It reruns
each method on random 80 percent subsamples and scores each setting by how well its clusters survive
resampling. chooseR then keeps the setting with the most clusters whose score clears a threshold, the
highest lower bound of a bootstrap interval over all candidates. We call this chooseR's reading, or
\emph{finest stable}, and also report the plain maximum of the same scores, or \emph{most stable}.
Labels are used only afterwards, to score the chosen partition. We first ran the rule at three seeds on
all ten scMINER benchmark datasets. A shared cap of 20 clusters turned out not to give the methods the same
candidate counts, so after seeing those results we added a matched design that offers every method the
same reachable counts, one setting each. We then fixed a protocol in advance, tagged it in our repository,
ran seven new seeds on the nine small datasets and five seeds on PBMC14K, and scored every run with
chooseR's own R code as well as ours. Last, we clustered all 2.3 million cells of the Allen whole mouse
brain atlas \cite{yao2023allen} with both PCA pipelines, and a sample of them with MICA.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{The candidates decide the ranking, and we show why.} On each method's own grid Leiden leads
MICA under the plain maximum, \V{S0.original.M.leiden} against \V{S0.original.M.mica} in mean adjusted Rand
index (ARI) over the nine small datasets, at every new seed. Offered the same counts, the two are level,
\V{S0.mid.M.mica} against \V{S0.mid.M.leiden}, because matching adds coarse counts that Leiden's own grid
never produced and the rule then picks. PCA plus $k$-means separates this effect of the menu from the
effect of the representation.
\item \emph{The rule's details move it too.} The reading moves MICA most, since its stability scores are
often flat at the top on datasets of a few hundred cells. The number of subsamples turns a level
comparison into a lead. chooseR's own code cannot form its interval when a coarse partition reproduces
perfectly, and then makes no choice, in \V{S3.nochoice.mica} MICA runs.
\item \emph{Which effects hold.} We sort the effects into those confirmed against criteria fixed in advance
and those only measured (Table~\ref{tab:effects}). MICA's lead under chooseR's reading holds at every seed,
\V{S0.mid.F.mica} against \V{S0.mid.F.leiden}, but the datasets split about evenly and with 25 subsamples
the lead rests on one of them.
\item \emph{Checks with and without labels.} We separate six checks a user can run on unlabeled data, which
ask whether a choice is determined by the data, from five that need labels and judge a benchmark's ranking
(Table~\ref{tab:checks}), and show on Pollen how the label-free ones flag a poor choice.
\item \emph{Scale.} On the full Allen atlas we report the dimensions, results, time and memory of both
baselines, and MICA's result from a 100,000-cell sample, kept apart from the full-data results.
\end{itemize}
