\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
A biologist who has sequenced a new tissue wants to know which cell types are in it. The
standard route is to cluster the cells and inspect the groups. Every clustering method used
for this has a setting that controls how finely it divides the data, and the result depends
on it strongly. On Zeisel, which has 7 annotated cell types, the baseline returns 2
clusters at the low end of its resolution grid and 21 at the setting its software ships
with. Choosing it is therefore not a
detail of tuning but the main decision, and it has to be made without knowing the answer,
because the number of cell types is usually what the experiment is trying to establish.

Published benchmarks do not make that decision. In the benchmark for scMINER
\cite{pan2025scminer}, the framework we study, every method is either given the true
number of cell types or has its setting swept and keeps only the runs reproducing that
number, with ties broken by agreement with the labels. The reported scores are
conditional on information a user does not have. This is not specific to scMINER:
Ullmann et al. \cite{ullmann2023overoptimistic} show that tuning against the ground
truth inflates a method's apparent advantage, and a recent survey
\cite{szmigiel2025benchmarking} reports that on six of sixteen datasets the common
default does no better than a random setting. What is missing is a direct measurement of
what happens to a specific published advantage when the selection is made without
labels.

\textbf{Main question.} When the same label-free rule chooses the setting for both methods,
does MICA, the mutual information clustering in scMINER, produce partitions closer to the
known cell types than a principal component and Leiden pipeline on the same genes?

Two supporting questions follow from it. How much of the answer is set by the rule rather
than by either method, and under what conditions does the answer hold?

Our approach holds everything except the method fixed. Both receive the same matrix,
choose among settings yielding 2 to 20 clusters, and are selected by the same rule. The
main rule is a published one, subsampling stability in the style of chooseR
\cite{pattersoncross2021chooser}, which reruns each method on random 80 percent
subsamples per candidate setting and prefers settings whose clusters survive resampling.
We use 25 subsamples where chooseR specifies 100, a reduction we justify and test in
Section~\ref{sec:design}. Labels are used only afterwards, to score what the rule chose.
The experiment covers all ten benchmark datasets at three seeds, with cost recorded.

The result is not the one we expected. MICA has the higher mean under three of the four
rules, which on its own would read as support for the published claim, but its mean moves
by 0.255 across those rules where the baseline's moves by 0.120. Two readings of one rule
reverse the ranking outright, with nothing about the data or either method differing
between them. The reason is measurable: among settings the rule scores as equivalent, the
span of cluster counts averages 7.3 for MICA against 2.7 for the baseline, so the
tie-break rather than the data usually decides. Our first explanation, that stability
would simply be less informative for MICA, was wrong, and a second prediction about
overclustering failed too.

The effect has a boundary, and we found it. On PBMC14K, the one dataset large enough to
put MICA in its graph mode, the ambiguity is absent: the tied settings all return the same
number of clusters, both readings agree, and MICA leads 0.629 to 0.397. Running both modes
on one fixed pool of 2,500 cells then shows it is the mode rather than the size that
matters. The failure belongs to MICA's small-data pipeline, which nine of the ten benchmark
datasets and most of the published claim rest on.

This submission makes two contributions.

\textbf{A measurement of what label-free selection costs, and of how unstable that cost
is.} The closest work establishes that benchmarks tuned on labels are optimistic
\cite{ullmann2023overoptimistic} and supplies rules meant to replace that tuning
\cite{pattersoncross2021chooser,liu2021multik}, but none has been applied to mutual
information clustering, and the scMINER benchmark reports no label-free result at all. We
supply that measurement, and find that the tie-break, the threshold and the random
subsample draw inside a single rule each move the result by as much as the gap between the
two methods.

\textbf{Evidence on why reproducibility fails as a criterion here, and on where it stops
failing.} MICA's partitions are unusually reproducible under subsampling, including those
that split a true cell type several ways, because its distance is computed per cell pair
and does not move when the sample changes. Splitting is then as repeatable as recovering,
and a rule rewarding repeatability cannot tell them apart. We state that property, verify
it as an exact identity, use it to make the experiment affordable, and show that the
ambiguity it produces is confined to the small-data mode.
