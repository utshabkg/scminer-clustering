\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
A biologist who has sequenced a new tissue wants to know which cell types are in it. The standard
route is to cluster the cells and inspect the groups, but every practical method has a setting that
controls how finely it divides the data. On Zeisel, which has 7 annotated cell types, a Leiden
pipeline returns 3 or 4 clusters at resolution 0.05, depending on the seed, and 20 or 21 at resolution
1.0, the setting Scanpy ships with. A user has to make that choice without knowing the answer,
because the number of cell types is usually what the experiment is trying to establish.

Benchmarks rarely face that choice. scMINER \cite{pan2025scminer} reports that its mutual information
clustering module, MICA, beats Seurat and Scanpy on average over ten labelled datasets, but MICA is run
at the true number of clusters and the graph methods are swept until they reproduce that count. Tuning
against the labels can make a method look better than it would to a user who does not have them
\cite{ullmann2023overoptimistic}. In single-cell benchmarks the way settings are chosen can reorder the
methods \cite{raimundo2020tuning}, and the cluster count a setting gives can matter more than the method
itself \cite{germain2020pipecomp}. A fair comparison therefore lets each method choose its own setting
without labels. That opens a second problem, and it is the subject of this paper: the label-free rule,
and the candidates it is shown, can decide the ranking too.

\textbf{Main question.} When one label-free rule chooses the setting for every method, how much of the
resulting ranking comes from the rule and its candidates rather than from the methods, and under what
conditions is the comparison reliable? We study it on MICA against two pipelines on the same genes,
principal component analysis (PCA) followed by Leiden, the usual single-cell route, and PCA followed by
$k$-means, the textbook baseline. Three supporting questions follow. Does offering every method the same
cluster counts change the ranking, and why? Do the rule's details, its reading, threshold and bootstrap,
change it? And how far does it move between independent subsample draws?

The rule is subsampling stability in the style of chooseR \cite{pattersoncross2021chooser}. It reruns
each method on random 80 percent subsamples and scores each setting by how well its clusters survive
resampling. chooseR then keeps the setting with the most clusters whose score clears a threshold, the
highest lower bound of a bootstrap interval over all candidates. We call this chooseR's reading, or
\emph{finest stable}, and also report the plain maximum of the same scores, or \emph{most stable}.
Labels are used only afterwards, to score the chosen partition. In Assignment 3 we ran the rule at three
seeds on all ten scMINER benchmark datasets. A shared cap of 20 clusters turned out not to give the
methods the same candidate counts, so after seeing those results we added a matched design that offers
every method the same reachable counts, one setting each. For this report we fixed the protocol in
advance and tagged it in our repository, then ran seven new seeds on the nine small datasets and seeds 0 to 4 on PBMC14K, scored every run with chooseR's own R code as well as ours, and added the $k$-means baseline.

\textbf{Findings.} The candidates decide the ranking under the plain maximum. On each method's own grid
Leiden leads MICA, \V{S0.original.M.leiden} against \V{S0.original.M.mica} in mean adjusted Rand index
(ARI) over the nine small datasets, and offered the same counts the two are level, \V{S0.mid.M.mica}
against \V{S0.mid.M.leiden}. Matching adds counts Leiden's own grid never produced, and the rule often
picks one of them. Under chooseR's reading MICA leads at every new seed, \V{S0.mid.F.mica} against
\V{S0.mid.F.leiden}, and the lead holds under chooseR's own code once its missing intervals are repaired
as the protocol declared, \V{S3G.mid.F.mica} against \V{S3G.mid.F.leiden}. Dataset by dataset, though,
the two methods split about evenly, and with 25 subsamples the mean lead rests on a single dataset. With
the menu of counts and the clustering step held fixed, MICA's mutual information representation beats the
principal components by \V{E2.mid.F.micaminuskmeans} under that reading. MICA's stability scores are
often flat at the top on datasets of a few hundred cells. Separately, chooseR's code as written makes no
choice at all in \V{S3.nochoice.mica} MICA runs, because a coarse partition that reproduces perfectly
leaves its interval undefined. On PBMC14K, the one large dataset, MICA and Leiden tie under the plain
maximum on matched candidates at \V{C4.ties} seeds, but the tie breaks when Leiden is offered its lowest setting per count. On the original grids there, the interval alone moves MICA's choice from
\V{pbmc.S2.original.F.mica.k} clusters to \V{pbmc.S3G.original.F.mica.k}.

From these results we draw conditions under which such a comparison can be trusted. The methods must
choose among the same cluster counts, and the ranking should not change with the setting behind each count. The two readings of
the rule should agree. The scores should not be flat at the top. And the ranking should hold across
subsample draws, across implementations of the rule and without any one dataset. Where one of these
fails, the comparison shows at most a lead under that rule on those datasets, not a ranking of the
methods. By these checks our benchmark establishes more about how the evaluation shapes a ranking than
about which method clusters better.
