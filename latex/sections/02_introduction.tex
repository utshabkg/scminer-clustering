\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
A biologist who has sequenced a new tissue wants to know which cell types are in it. The
standard route is to cluster the cells and inspect the groups. Every clustering method used
for this has a setting that controls how finely it divides the data, and the result depends
on it strongly: on Zeisel, which has 7
annotated cell types, MICA returns between 7 and 59 clusters as that setting moves across
its own default range, and Leiden between 1 and 33. Choosing it is therefore not a
detail of tuning but the main decision, and it has to be made without knowing the answer,
because the number of cell types is usually what the experiment is trying to establish.

Published benchmarks do not make that decision. In the benchmark for scMINER
\cite{pan2025scminer}, the framework we study, every method is either given the true
number of cell types or has its setting swept and keeps only the runs reproducing that
number, with ties broken by agreement with the labels. The reported scores are
conditional on information a user does not have. This is not specific to scMINER:
Ullmann et al. \cite{ullmann2023overoptimistic} show that tuning against the ground
truth inflates a method's apparent advantage, and a recent survey
\cite{szmigiel2025benchmarking} reports that on six of sixteen datasets the common
default does no better than a random setting. What is missing is a direct measurement of
what happens to a specific published advantage when the selection is made without
labels.

\textbf{Main question.} When the same label-free rule chooses the setting for both methods,
does MICA, the mutual information clustering in scMINER, produce partitions closer to the
known cell types than a principal component and Leiden pipeline on the same genes?

Our approach holds everything except the method fixed. Both receive the same matrix,
choose among settings yielding 2 to 20 clusters, and are selected by the same rule. The
main rule is a published one, subsampling stability as specified by chooseR
\cite{pattersoncross2021chooser}, which reruns each method on 25 random 80 percent
subsamples per candidate setting and prefers settings whose clusters survive resampling.
Labels are used only afterwards, to score what the rule chose. The experiment covers
nine of the ten benchmark datasets, three seeds, with cost recorded.

The result is not the one we expected. MICA has the higher mean under three of the four
rules we applied, which on its own would read as support for the published claim. But
its mean ranges from 0.473 to 0.727 across those rules, a spread of 0.255, where the
baseline's is 0.120. Two readings of one rule reverse the ranking outright: MICA leads
0.661 to 0.632 when chooseR is read as its authors specify, and trails 0.473 to 0.635
when the same scores are read with a different tie-break. Nothing about the data or
either method differs between them.
We traced this to a measurable property: among settings the rule scores as equivalent,
the span of cluster counts averages 7.3 for MICA and 2.7 for the baseline, so for MICA
the tie-break rather than the data usually decides. On one dataset the rule cannot
distinguish 3 clusters from 19. Our initial explanation, that stability would simply be less informative for MICA,
turned out to be wrong: it correlates slightly better with accuracy for MICA than for
the baseline. The signal is present but compressed into a band too narrow for a
threshold to act on. A further prediction we drew from that account also failed its
test, and we report it as failed.

This submission makes two contributions.

\textbf{A measurement of what label-free selection costs, and of how unstable that cost is.}
The closest work establishes that benchmarks which tune on the labels are optimistic
\cite{ullmann2023overoptimistic}, and supplies rules intended to replace that tuning
\cite{pattersoncross2021chooser,liu2021multik}, but no study applies such a rule to mutual
information clustering, and the scMINER benchmark reports no label-free result at all. We supply that measurement and find that three incidental choices inside a single rule,
its tie-break, its threshold and its random subsample draw, each move the result by as
much as the gap between the two methods. A benchmark reporting one label-free rule could
obtain either ranking.

\textbf{An account of why reproducibility fails as a criterion for this method.} MICA's
partitions are unusually reproducible under subsampling, including those that split a
true cell type several ways, because its distance is computed per cell pair and does not
move when the sample changes. Splitting is then as repeatable as recovering, and a rule
rewarding repeatability cannot tell them apart. We state the property behind this,
verify it as an exact identity, and use it to make the experiment affordable.
