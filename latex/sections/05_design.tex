\section{Experimental Design}\label{sec:design}
The experiment asks one question: when the same label-free rule picks the setting for
both methods, which method's chosen partition agrees better with the reference labels?
The comparison is paired. For every dataset and rule, the input matrix, the gene set,
the candidate range and the scoring are fixed, and only the method differs.

\subsection{Data}
We use the ten labelled datasets of the scMINER benchmark in the processed form the
authors released, so differences from their reported numbers cannot come from our
preprocessing. They span 124 to 13,605 cells and 3 to 11 cell types, the range over
which the published claim was made, and are listed with their sizes, label counts and
sparsity in Table~\ref{tab:data}.



Two of them carry most of the weight. PBMC14K is the only dataset whose labels are
independent of clustering, since its populations were sorted with antibodies before
sequencing \cite{zheng2017massively}. Zeisel's labels came from the original authors
clustering the same data \cite{zeisel2015cell}, so any method resembling theirs is
flattered. We say which is which whenever a conclusion rests on one of them.

\subsection{What is held fixed}
Both methods receive the same matrix with all genes retained, and both choose among
settings whose full-data partition has 2 to 20 clusters, so neither can win by searching
a wider range. The true number of types is never supplied. Both see the same $B = 25$
subsamples within a seed, which makes the comparison paired at the level of the draw.

\subsection{Use of labels}
Labels are used in two places only. They score the chosen partition, after selection.
And they define the label-informed reference, which reproduces the published protocol by
letting the labels pick the setting. That is an upper bound on what each method could
achieve, not a result a user could obtain.

\subsection{Runs and variation}
Every stochastic comparison is repeated. The stability rule is run with three independent
seeds, which redraw the subsamples and reinitialise the clustering, and we report the
median and the full range across those runs. Within a run, MICA's $k$-means uses 50
restarts, enough to reach its own optimum reliably. Those are restarts inside one run and
are not counted as repeats. Leiden is run at three seeds per setting under the fixed-setting
protocols. Where a range is reported it is the observed minimum and maximum, not a
confidence interval, because three runs are too few to justify one.

\subsection{Measures}
Agreement with the labels is the adjusted Rand index (ARI) and the adjusted mutual
information (AMI). We report both because ARI penalises splitting a true type across
clusters more sharply than AMI does, so a gap between them is itself evidence about how a
method is failing. We also report $|P_{\hat{s}}|$, the number of clusters the rule chose,
because a rule can reach a respectable ARI with a badly wrong number of clusters, and a
user would notice the latter.

\subsection{Cost}
Cost is wall-clock time on one shared workstation with 120 cores and 754 GB of memory,
every job capped at 8 threads and no GPU used. It counts everything a rule needs: the
candidate partitions, the subsample reruns and the scoring. MICA's one-off distance
matrix is shared across all settings and seeds and is reported separately. We also give
the number of clustering calls, as a hardware-independent companion to the timings.
