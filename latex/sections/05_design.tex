\section{Experimental Design}\label{sec:design}
The comparison is paired. For every dataset and rule, the input matrix, the gene set and the
scoring are fixed, and the method and its grid differ.

\textbf{Data.} We use the ten labelled datasets of the scMINER benchmark in the processed form the authors
released, with all genes retained for both methods (Table~\ref{tab:data}). They span 124 to 13,605
cells and 3 to 11 cell types. PBMC14K's labels are cell types fixed by antibody sorting before
sequencing \cite{zheng2017massively}. Several small datasets are labelled by experimental condition,
while Zeisel's labels came from the original authors clustering the same data \cite{zeisel2015cell},
which flatters any method resembling theirs.

\textbf{The runs as first specified.} The protocol was fixed before any partition was scored: the candidate grids, 25 subsamples of 80
percent at three seeds, and the selection step. chooseR's reading is primary, and the plain maximum, specified with it, is a second rule. Both methods choose among settings whose full-data
partition has 2 to 20 clusters and see the same subsamples within a seed. That caps the range without
matching it: MICA's $k$ covers all of it on every dataset, but the baseline's resolution grid of 0.05
to 2.0 never does. chooseR specifies $B = 100$. We use 25 because the rule costs $B + 1$ clusterings
per candidate setting, so 100 would take roughly four times the compute. Fewer subsamples may widen the
bootstrap intervals and so lower the threshold. We did not rerun at 100. We test how sensitive the
selection is to the threshold instead, which is a related check and not the same comparison.

\textbf{Matched candidates.}\label{sec:matcheddesign} Restricting MICA's saved scores to the counts the baseline produced (Table~\ref{tab:common}) suggested
the unmatched range mattered, but that restriction was chosen after the results were seen and applied
to MICA only. We therefore fixed a matched design before running it. In each run every method is offered
the counts from 2 to 20 that all of them reach on the full data, with one setting per count, the middle
of the settings in grid order that give it. One per count matters because the threshold is the highest
lower bound among the candidates, and a dense grid would give the baseline several chances at it for
every one of MICA's. Label-free probes on the full data showed that no resolution grid reaches every count: 300 log-spaced Leiden resolutions from 0.001 to 50 still skip up to 5 of the 19 counts in a
run, and MICA's graph mode never gives fewer than 4 clusters on PBMC14K, even at a resolution of 30. The
rule, the subsamples and the seeds are unchanged, and the silhouette rule gets the same candidates.

\textbf{Use of labels.}\label{sec:labeluse} Labels are used in four places, none of which feeds a label-free choice. They score the chosen
partition, they fix the setting in the reproduction of the published protocol, and they pick the
resolution in the graph comparison so that only the graph differs. The transfer comparison also uses
them, so it is reported apart: each small dataset takes the setting with the best mean ARI over the
other eight at the same seed, with ties going to the smaller setting.

\textbf{Runs, measures and cost.} The stability rule is run with three independent seeds, which redraw the subsamples and reinitialise the
clustering, and we report the median with the full range of chosen cluster counts. MICA's $k$-means uses
50 restarts within a run, which are restarts rather than repeats. The default rule ran at three seeds and the silhouette rule at one, so it shows no variation. A range is the observed minimum and
maximum, not a confidence interval, because three runs are too few to justify one.

Agreement with the labels is the ARI, with the adjusted mutual information (AMI) in Table~\ref{tab:ami}
and in the text for the matched runs. We also report the number of clusters the rule chose, because a
rule can reach a respectable ARI with a badly wrong count. Cost is wall-clock time on one workstation with
120 cores and 754 GB, every job on 8 threads and no GPU. Selection counts the candidate partitions, the
subsample reruns and the scoring, and is reported apart from the cost of clustering once.

\textbf{Graph comparison.} The faster neighbour graph is compared with MICA's own on PBMC14K and on 100,000 cells drawn at random
from the 10x v3 data of the Allen whole mouse brain atlas \cite{yao2023allen}, both fed to MICA's own
node2vec and Louvain so that only the graph differs. We preprocessed these Allen cells ourselves from the
raw counts ($\log_2(\mathrm{CPM}+1)$, 28,536 genes after filtering), and score them against the atlas's 34
cell classes, which its authors derived by clustering. The shortlist size follows one stated rule: the
smallest value on a doubling grid whose recall on 500 brute-forced query cells reaches that of MICA's search.
We wrote it after Assignment 2 had picked 800 and 3,200 from the same recall audits, and it gives those
counts back. Setting it uses no labels.
