\section{Experimental Design}\label{sec:design}
The comparison is paired. For every dataset and rule, the input matrix, the gene set, the cap
on the candidate range and the scoring are fixed, and the method and its grid differ.

\subsection{Data}
We use the ten labelled datasets of the scMINER benchmark in the processed form the
authors released, so differences from their reported numbers cannot come from our
preprocessing. They span 124 to 13,605 cells and 3 to 11 cell types, the range over which
the published claim was made (Table~\ref{tab:data}). PBMC14K's labels are cell types fixed by
antibody sorting before sequencing \cite{zheng2017massively}. Several small datasets are
labelled by experimental condition, such as cell-cycle phase on Buettner, culture medium on
Kolodziejczyk and time point on Klein, while Zeisel's labels came from the original authors
clustering the same data \cite{zeisel2015cell}, which flatters any method resembling theirs.

\subsection{What is held fixed}
Both methods receive the same matrix with all genes retained. In the runs as first specified,
both choose among settings whose full-data partition has 2 to 20 clusters. That caps the range
without matching it: MICA's $k$ covers all of it on every dataset, but the baseline's
resolution grid of 0.05 to 2.0 never reaches all of it. The matched runs below fix
that. The true number of types is never supplied. Both see the same $B = 25$ subsamples within
a seed, which makes the comparison paired at the level of the draw.

chooseR specifies $B = 100$. We use 25 because the rule costs $B + 1$ clusterings per
candidate setting, so 100 would take roughly four times the compute for every result here.
The reduction widens the bootstrap intervals and lowers the threshold, which could change
which settings clear it, so rather than assume it is harmless we test the selection's
sensitivity to the threshold directly in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

For the runs as first specified, the protocol was fixed before any partition was scored: the
candidate grids, 25 subsamples of 80 percent at three seeds, and the selection step. The
matched grids came later (Section~\ref{sec:matcheddesign}). chooseR's own reading is the primary
rule. The plain maximum, specified with it and computed from the same scores in the same run,
is a second rule, and comparing the two measures how much the result depends on the
tie-break.

\subsection{Matched candidates}\label{sec:matcheddesign}
Restricting MICA's saved scores to the counts the baseline produced (Table~\ref{tab:common})
suggested the unmatched range mattered, but that restriction was chosen after the results were
seen and applied to MICA only. We therefore fixed a matched design before running it. Two probes
on the full data, which read no labels and score nothing, showed that no fixed grid reaches
every count: 300 log-spaced Leiden resolutions from 0.001 to 50 still skip up to 5 of the 19
counts in a run, because Leiden jumps over some counts as the resolution moves, and MICA's
graph-mode Louvain never gives fewer than 4 clusters on PBMC14K, even at a resolution of 30. So
in each run every method is offered the counts from 2 to 20 that all of them reach on the full
data, with one setting per count, the middle of the settings in grid order that give it. One
per count matters because the threshold is the highest lower bound among the candidates, and a
dense grid would give the baseline several chances at it for every one of MICA's. The baseline
sweeps the 300-point grid, MICA keeps $k$ from 2 to 20 in small-data mode, and in graph mode
its Louvain runs in steps of 0.01 up to 2.0 and more coarsely up to 30. The rule, the
subsamples and the seeds are unchanged, and the silhouette rule gets the same matched
candidates. We report the runs as first specified and the matched runs side by side, and the
matched design was chosen after the post hoc check.

\subsection{Use of labels}\label{sec:labeluse}
Labels are used in four places, none of which feeds a label-free choice. They score the
chosen partition, they fix the setting in the reproduction of the published protocol, an
upper bound rather than a result a user could obtain, and they pick the resolution in the
graph comparison so that only the graph differs. The transfer comparison also uses them, so
it is not label-free and is reported apart: each small dataset takes the setting with the best
mean ARI over the other eight at the same seed, $k$ for MICA and a resolution on the 300-point
grid for the baseline, with ties going to the smaller setting. PBMC14K takes the baseline's
resolution that is best on the nine. MICA's graph mode has no setting that carries over from
$k$, so MICA is not transferred there. The Assignment 2 plan proposed this rule in graph mode
on Klein, Zeisel and PBMC14K. Transferring $k$ across the nine small datasets instead compares
like with like and also tests MICA's shipped default.

\subsection{Runs and variation}
Every stochastic comparison is repeated. The stability rule, as first specified and on matched
candidates, is run with three independent seeds, which redraw the subsamples and reinitialise the clustering, and we report the
median across those runs with the full range of chosen cluster counts. Per-dataset ARI
ranges are in \texttt{results/a3\_seed\_variation.csv} for the runs as first specified and in
\texttt{results/a3\_matched\_seed\_variation.csv} for the matched runs. MICA's $k$-means uses 50 restarts
within a run, enough to reach its own optimum reliably, which are restarts rather than
repeats. The default rule was run at three seeds and the silhouette rule, which involves no
resampling, at one.
Where a range is reported it is the observed minimum and maximum, not a confidence
interval, because three runs are too few to justify one.

\subsection{Measures}
Agreement with the labels is the ARI in the main table. The adjusted mutual information is in
Table~\ref{tab:ami} for the rules as first specified and in Sections~\ref{sec:matched}
and~\ref{sec:pbmc} for the matched runs. The ARI is the stricter of the two here in both directions: across MICA's 54
stability picks on the original grids the AMI sits above it by 0.17 on average when the
rule chose too many clusters and by 0.12 when it chose too few, which it did in 35 of them. We also report $|P_{\hat{s}}|$, the number of clusters the rule chose, because a
rule can reach a respectable ARI with a badly wrong number of clusters, and a user would
notice the latter.

\subsection{Cost}
We report the cost of selecting a setting apart from the cost of clustering once, since a
user pays both. Cost is wall-clock time on one workstation with 120 cores and 754 GB, every
job on 8 threads and no GPU. Selection counts the candidate partitions, the subsample reruns
and the scoring. MICA's one-off distance matrix and the number of clustering calls are given
separately. In the matched runs the timer started after the full-data sweep that fixes the
shared counts, so those times leave that sweep out, and we give its duration from the log
timestamps.

\subsection{Graph comparison}
The faster neighbour graph is compared with MICA's own on PBMC14K and on 100,000 cells of the
Allen brain atlas, both fed to MICA's own node2vec and Louvain so that only the graph differs.
The candidate count follows one stated rule: the smallest value on a doubling grid whose
recall on 500 brute-forced query cells reaches that of MICA's search. We wrote it after
Assignment 2 had picked 800 and 3,200 from the same recall audits, and it gives those counts
back. It stays fixed for any further dataset. Recall here is
agreement with exact neighbours, so setting it uses no labels.
