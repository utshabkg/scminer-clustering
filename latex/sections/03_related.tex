\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
Four groups of work bear on our question. MICA is the method under test, stability rules supply the
label-free choice, studies of tuning show how a ranking can depend on it, and single-cell benchmarks
show how settings decide scores on cells.

\textbf{The method under test.} scMINER \cite{pan2025scminer} introduces MICA and reports a mean ARI of
0.84 across ten labelled datasets, against 0.73 for Seurat and 0.68 for Scanpy. MICA measures cell-cell
similarity with binned mutual information. Below about 5,000 cells it uses multidimensional scaling
followed by $k$-means, and larger inputs use a mutual information neighbour graph, node2vec and Louvain.
Its headline scores use the true number of cell types, and the graph-based comparisons sweep settings
until the cluster count matches the truth. The benchmark therefore leaves open how MICA fares when the
setting must be chosen without labels, which makes it a useful test case for our question.

\textbf{Choosing the cluster count without labels.} chooseR \cite{pattersoncross2021chooser} repeatedly
clusters 80 percent subsamples, measures how robustly cells co-cluster and keeps the finest partition
that stays above a stability threshold. MultiK \cite{liu2021multik} also uses subsampling and consensus,
but returns several plausible values of $K$. Yu et al. \cite{yu2022benchmarking} benchmarked 14 ways of
estimating the number of cell types and found that good agreement with the labels does not imply a
correct count. sc-SHC \cite{grabski2023significance} and CHOIR \cite{sant2025choir} instead test whether
a split is real, against a fitted null or with a classifier. Stability-based choice of $k$ is older than single-cell
data \cite{BenHur2002Stability,Lange2004Stability}, and from the start it broke ties at the top by
taking the largest stable $k$ \cite{BenHur2002Stability,Tibshirani2005Prediction}, as chooseR does. Its
limits are also well known. A clustering can be perfectly stable without being right \cite{BenDavid2006Sober},
stability becomes trivial when the perturbation is too small to change the result
\cite{vonLuxburg2010Stability}, and consensus clustering finds stable clusters in data that have none
\cite{Senbabaoglu2014Critical}. Haslbeck and Wulff \cite{Haslbeck2020Corrected} show that instability
depends on the number of clusters itself, so what a stability rule picks depends on which values of $k$
it is allowed to see. None of these studies compares methods whose settings reach different cluster
counts, which is what a resolution parameter does.

\textbf{How tuning changes rankings.} Outside clustering, rankings are known to depend on how each
method is tuned. Melis et al. \cite{melis2018state} found that plain LSTMs beat newer language models
once every model had the same tuning, and Lucic et al. \cite{lucic2018gans} found no GAN clearly better
than another under equal search budgets. Dodge et al. \cite{dodge2019show} found published winners that
hold only for some tuning budgets, and Bouthillier et al. \cite{bouthillier2021accounting} found that the
tuning procedure adds as much variance as weight initialisation, with the range and step of a grid an
arbitrary source of it. For clustering, Mishra et al. \cite{mishra2022evaluative} rank methods by the
best ARI reached after a given number of tuning trials and find that the winner depends on how much
tuning each method gets. Ullmann et al. \cite{ullmann2023overoptimistic} show that tuning against known
labels inflates a new method's apparent advantage, and the white paper of Van Mechelen et al.
\cite{vanmechelen2023white} asks that a benchmark give every method the same information and an equally
fair choice of tuning parameters, and report every value tried. Apart from the white paper, these studies either choose settings with labels or study tasks other than clustering. None asks how a label-free selection rule changes a clustering
ranking.

\textbf{Settings in single-cell clustering benchmarks.} On cells, the cluster count dominates the score.
Germain et al. \cite{germain2020pipecomp} found the number of clusters called to be the main determinant
of ARI, well ahead of the clustering method, and Krzak et al. \cite{krzak2019benchmark} found that
several methods depend strongly on their settings and that most do better when given the true $k$.
Raimundo et al. \cite{raimundo2020tuning} showed that choosing settings by default, with the labels, or
with a silhouette heuristic reorders dimensionality reduction methods, though the heuristic was still
given the true $k$. Freytag et al. \cite{freytag2018comparison} ran every tool at its defaults, so most chose $k$ by their own rule,
which mixes the algorithm with its selection rule, and Visser and Datta \cite{visser2025integrating}
found that stability and internal measures disagree on the best count and on the winning pipeline.
Szmigiel et al. \cite{szmigiel2025benchmarking} show that Leiden's and Louvain's software defaults can be
weak or arbitrary. Rankings do not always move. Du{\`o} et al. \cite{duo2018systematic} found SC3 among the best methods at the true $k$ and still the best at its own estimate of $k$, so a reversal has to be shown case by case. We hold one label-free rule fixed across methods, change its candidates and its details
one at a time, and measure what each change does to the ranking.

\textbf{Outside our scope.} Information-theoretic clustering can reach millions of cells: minicore
\cite{baker2021minicore} uses divergences such as Jensen-Shannon rather than mutual information between
cell profiles. Approximate search with exact rescoring \cite{malkov2020efficient,douze2024faiss}
motivates the faster MICA graph in Appendix~\ref{app:graph}. Language models now name clusters from
marker genes and retrieved knowledge \cite{hou2024gpt4,crowley2025anndictionary,xie2025cassia}, but they
take the partition as given, which is why choosing it comes first.
