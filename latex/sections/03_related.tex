\section{Closest Work}\label{sec:related}
Three bodies of work bound this study: the method we test, the rules we use to select a
clustering without labels, and the argument that benchmarks which tune on the ground truth
overstate what users get. We treat each in the detail needed to see what is already
established and what remains.

\subsection{The method under test}
scMINER \cite{pan2025scminer} introduces MICA and reports a mean ARI of 0.84 across ten
labelled datasets, against 0.73 for Seurat and 0.68 for Scanpy. Every setting in that
benchmark is fixed with the labels, and nine of the ten datasets sit below the 5,000
cell threshold, so nine of the ten headline scores come from MICA given the true number
of clusters. In the authors' own benchmark files MICA trails Scanpy on the three larger
datasets, which were used for runtime rather than accuracy. Asked during peer review how
a user should choose the resolution without labels, the authors pointed to silhouette
scores and marker genes but tested neither, and MICA's shipped defaults appear only in
its source code. We found no independent benchmark that includes MICA, so its behaviour
outside the published protocol was open before this work.

\subsection{Choosing a clustering without labels}
chooseR \cite{pattersoncross2021chooser} is the rule we adopt. It reruns a pipeline on 100
random 80 percent subsamples per candidate setting, scores each setting by how reliably
cells co-cluster, and keeps the finest setting that stays above a threshold derived from
the settings themselves. It was demonstrated on Seurat and scVI pipelines at roughly 11,000
cells, where it selected 29 and 30 clusters, and the authors note that preferring the
finest acceptable setting leans toward mild overclustering. It has not been applied to a
mutual information method, and its behaviour on datasets of a few hundred cells was not
examined. Both gaps matter here, since nine of our ten datasets are smaller than the ones
it was shown on.

MultiK \cite{liu2021multik} pools 4,000 subsampled Seurat runs by the number of clusters
they produce and returns two or three candidate counts rather than one. Yu et al.
\cite{yu2022benchmarking} benchmarked 14 methods that estimate the number of cell types
and found that most underestimate it, with the error growing as the true number rises.
One conclusion bears directly on our design: a good ARI did not imply a correct cluster
count, which is why we report the chosen count beside the score. None of these includes
a mutual information method.

A second family tests whether a split is real rather than reproducible. sc-SHC
\cite{grabski2023significance} compares each split against a one-population null model,
and on data from a single cell line returned one cluster where Seurat's Louvain and
Leiden defaults both returned nine. CHOIR \cite{sant2025choir} overclusters, then merges
neighbouring clusters a random forest cannot separate better than chance. The
distinction matters here: a partition can be perfectly reproducible without being real.

\subsection{Tuning on the ground truth}
Ullmann et al. \cite{ullmann2023overoptimistic} set out to make a recently published
clustering algorithm look good by tuning its hyperparameters and the data settings
against the known labels, then showed how much of that advantage disappeared when the
choices moved. They state that tuning on the ground truth is common in the literature
and overstates what a user without labels obtains, and recommend tuning on separate
datasets. Their study used toy and UCI data and one algorithm, with no single-cell data
and no label-free rule tested as the remedy. A
2025 preprint \cite{szmigiel2025benchmarking} swept Leiden and Louvain over a very large
grid on 16 single-cell datasets and reports that on six of them the Scanpy default did no
better than a typical random setting, which is evidence that the default is a weak rule but
not a study of label-free selection either.

Our question sits in the gap these three lines leave. Ullmann et al. establish the bias in
principle. chooseR and its relatives supply rules but have not been applied to mutual
information clustering, and the scMINER benchmark reports no label-free result at all. What
is missing is a direct measurement: hold the rule fixed, apply it identically to both
methods, and see what survives.

\subsection{Scaling the neighbour graph} The secondary question is unchanged since
Assignment 2. Proposing candidate neighbours cheaply and rescoring them exactly is
standard in nearest neighbour search, as in the refinement step of Faiss
\cite{douze2024faiss}, and MICA itself uses HNSW \cite{malkov2020efficient}. Song et al.
\cite{song2012comparison} found that binned mutual information tracks correlation
closely for gene pairs, which is what suggested correlation as the cheap stage.
