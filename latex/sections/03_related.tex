\section{Closest Work}\label{sec:related}
Three bodies of work bound this study: the method we test, the rules we use to select a
clustering without labels, and the argument that benchmarks which tune on the ground truth
overstate what users get. We treat each in the detail needed to see what is already
established and what remains.

\subsection{The method under test}
scMINER \cite{pan2025scminer} introduces MICA and reports a mean ARI of 0.84 across ten
labelled datasets, against 0.73 for Seurat and 0.68 for Scanpy. The setting of every method
in that benchmark is fixed with the labels, as described in Section~\ref{sec:method}, and
nine of the ten datasets are below the 5,000 cell threshold, so nine of the ten headline
scores come from MICA given the true number of clusters. In the authors' own benchmark
files MICA trails Scanpy on the three larger datasets, between 76,000 and 647,000 cells,
which were used for runtime and memory rather than accuracy. The paper reports no setting
chosen without labels. Asked during peer review how a user should choose the resolution
when labels are unavailable, the authors pointed to silhouette scores and marker genes but
did not test either. MICA's shipped default resolution appears only in the source code,
with no derivation given. We found no independent benchmark that includes MICA, so its
behaviour outside the published protocol was open before this work.

\subsection{Choosing a clustering without labels}
chooseR \cite{pattersoncross2021chooser} is the rule we adopt. It reruns a pipeline on 100
random 80 percent subsamples per candidate setting, scores each setting by how reliably
cells co-cluster, and keeps the finest setting that stays above a threshold derived from
the settings themselves. It was demonstrated on Seurat and scVI pipelines at roughly 11,000
cells, where it selected 29 and 30 clusters, and the authors note that preferring the
finest acceptable setting leans toward mild overclustering. It has not been applied to a
mutual information method, and its behaviour on datasets of a few hundred cells was not
examined. Both gaps matter here, since nine of our ten datasets are smaller than the ones
it was shown on.

MultiK \cite{liu2021multik} takes a related approach, pooling 4,000 subsampled Seurat runs
by the number of clusters they produce and returning two or three candidate counts rather
than one. Yu et al. \cite{yu2022benchmarking} benchmarked 14 methods that estimate the
number of cell types on mixtures of Tabula Muris cells, and found that most underestimate
it, with the error growing as the true number rises, while SC3 and Seurat overestimate. One
of their conclusions bears directly on our design: a good ARI did not imply a correct
cluster count, which is why we report the chosen count alongside the score. None of these
studies includes a mutual information method.

A second family tests whether a split is real rather than whether it is reproducible.
sc-SHC \cite{grabski2023significance} compares each split of a hierarchy against a
one-population null model, and on data from a single cell line it returned one cluster
where Seurat's Louvain and Leiden defaults both returned nine. CHOIR \cite{sant2025choir}
overclusters first, then merges neighbouring clusters a random forest cannot separate
better than chance, and matched or beat 15 methods on 230 simulated datasets. The
distinction matters for our result: reproducibility and significance are different
criteria, and a partition can be perfectly reproducible without being real.

\subsection{Tuning on the ground truth}
Ullmann et al. \cite{ullmann2023overoptimistic} set out to make a recently published
clustering algorithm look good, by tuning its hyperparameters and the data settings against
the known labels, and then showed how much of the advantage disappeared when those choices
moved. They state that tuning hyperparameters on the ground truth is frequently seen in the
literature and overstates what a user without labels will obtain, and they recommend tuning
on separate datasets and testing on held-out ones. Their study used toy and UCI data and a
single algorithm, with no single-cell data and no label-free rule tested as the remedy. A
2025 preprint \cite{szmigiel2025benchmarking} swept Leiden and Louvain over a very large
grid on 16 single-cell datasets and reports that on six of them the Scanpy default did no
better than a typical random setting, which is evidence that the default is a weak rule but
not a study of label-free selection either.

Our question sits in the gap these three lines leave. Ullmann et al. establish the bias in
principle; chooseR and its relatives supply rules but have not been applied to mutual
information clustering; and the scMINER benchmark reports no label-free result at all. What
is missing is a direct measurement: hold the rule fixed, apply it identically to both
methods, and see what survives.

\subsection{Scaling the neighbour graph}
The secondary question is unchanged since Assignment 2 and is summarised here. Proposing
candidate neighbours cheaply and rescoring them exactly is standard in nearest neighbour
search, as in the refinement step of Faiss \cite{douze2024faiss}, and MICA itself uses HNSW
\cite{malkov2020efficient}. Song et al. \cite{song2012comparison} found that binned mutual
information tracks correlation closely for gene pairs, which is what suggested correlation
as the cheap stage. Amblard et al. \cite{amblard2022hubness} showed that single-cell
neighbour graphs contain hub cells and that correcting for them improves Leiden clustering,
although they tuned the resolution to the true number of clusters.
